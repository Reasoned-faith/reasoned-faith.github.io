<h2>Introduction</h2>

<p>The following article is based on the introduction to Genesis in Gleason L. Archer Jr., <em>A Survey of Old Testament Introduction</em> (Chicago: Moody Press), pp. 193-195. It shows that Genesis is not a random pile of ancient fragments but a carefully planned book of origins, and that its title, its authorship, its outline and even its repeated use of a single Hebrew word point to one guiding mind. This should trouble critics who treat Genesis as a patchwork of competing sources, since the structure of the book itself argues against them.</p>

<p>With that said, I proceed to the summary.</p>

<h2>Main Discussion</h2>

<p>Each part below builds on the one before it, so it is best read in order.</p>

<h3>1. Why Begin with the Question of Authorship?</h3>

<p>Before we ask what Genesis says about creation, the flood or the age of man, we have to ask a more basic question: what kind of book is it? Who wrote it, and does it come from one mind or from many?</p>

<p>The answer shapes everything that follows. If Genesis is a late collage of unrelated stories, its opening chapters can be read as folklore. If it is a unified work, written for a purpose and rooted in real family records, then its opening chapters must be read as history with a message. That is why this series starts here.</p>

<h3>2. The Title: A Book of Origins</h3>

<p>The name <em>Genesis</em> is Greek and means “beginning.” It was given to the book by the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament. The Hebrew title is simply the opening word of the book, <em>bereshit</em>, “in the beginning” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A1&version=ESV">Genesis 1:1</a>).</p>

<p>Both titles fit the contents. Genesis is about origins: the origin of the created world, of the human race, of the nations of the earth, and above all of the covenant family that makes up the redeemed people of God. Every major theme of the Bible, from sin and judgment to promise and redemption, is first planted here.</p>

<h3>3. Who Wrote Genesis?</h3>

<p>The book does not name its author. Tradition, however, points to Moses, and there are four reasons to take that tradition seriously.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The New Testament treats Genesis as part of the law of Moses.</strong> Circumcision on the eighth day is first introduced in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+17%3A12&version=ESV">Genesis 17:12</a>, repeated in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus+12%3A48&version=ESV">Exodus 12:48</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus+12%3A3&version=ESV">Leviticus 12:3</a>, and in John 7:23 Jesus speaks of it as belonging to the law of Moses. The practice is traced back through the same body of writing.</li>
  <li><strong>Exodus needs Genesis.</strong> The promises made to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob are spelled out in Genesis, and the rest of the Torah keeps referring to them as fulfilled in the Exodus and the conquest of Canaan. Without Genesis, those promises would have no beginning.</li>
  <li><strong>Exodus begins with “and.”</strong> The Hebrew of <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus+1%3A1&version=ESV">Exodus 1:1</a> opens with a connecting word, which suggests it was written to follow an earlier book, not to stand alone.</li>
  <li><strong>Moses had a reason to write it.</strong> As he prepared a constitution for the people of God, Israel needed an accurate record of its own national origin in Abraham and in God’s covenant dealings with him and his descendants. A nation about to enter the land needed to know why it was there.</li>
</ul>

<p>Together these four points do not prove the case like a mathematical theorem, but they form a consistent picture: the tradition of Mosaic authorship fits what the Bible says about itself and what the situation of Israel required.</p>

<h3>4. Where Did Moses Get His Material?</h3>

<p>Moses lived long after the events of Genesis. The materials he used probably came to him from five or six centuries before his time, from before Jacob went down into Egypt. The best picture is that Moses acted as a Spirit-guided compiler and interpreter of older material handed down by his forefathers in oral and written form.</p>

<p>This matters because it answers a common objection. Saying that Moses wrote Genesis does not mean he invented every detail from nothing, or that he sat down with a blank page. It means that under the guidance of the Spirit he gathered, arranged and interpreted the family records of the patriarchs into one purposeful book.</p>

<h3>5. The Shape of the Book</h3>

<p>Genesis can be outlined in five movements:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Section I</strong> (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A1-11%3A32&version=ESV">1:1 – 11:32</a>): The beginning of mankind: creation, the fall, the flood, the early nations</li>
  <li><strong>Section II</strong> (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+12%3A1-25%3A18&version=ESV">12:1 – 25:18</a>): The life of Abraham</li>
  <li><strong>Section III</strong> (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+25%3A19-26%3A35&version=ESV">25:19 – 26:35</a>): The life of Isaac and his family</li>
  <li><strong>Section IV</strong> (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+27%3A1-37%3A1&version=ESV">27:1 – 37:1</a>): The life of Jacob</li>
  <li><strong>Section V</strong> (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+37%3A2-50%3A26&version=ESV">37:2 – 50:26</a>): The life of Joseph</li>
</ul>

<p>The pattern is careful and deliberate. The thread running through it is the <strong>covenant of grace</strong>, and God’s gracious dealings with believers from Adam onward.</p>

<p>First the circle narrows by stages to one man, Abraham. Then the principle of election widens to include a whole family, the family of Jacob. And so the stage is set for the growth of a nation in the fertile refuge of Goshen in Egypt.</p>

<p>Notice the movement: from all humanity, to one man, to one family, to one nation. Genesis is not wandering through unrelated stories. It is following a single path.</p>

<h3>6. Evidence for a Single Author</h3>

<p>Some scholars follow the Documentary Hypothesis, the theory that the Torah was stitched together from several originally separate sources. Two features of Genesis support the opposite view, that it comes from one mind with one plan.</p>

<h4>The word <em>toledot</em></h4>

<p>The Hebrew word <em>toledot</em> (“generations,” “offspring,” “descendants”) introduces most of the main sections of Genesis: at <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+2%3A4&version=ESV">2:4</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+5%3A1&version=ESV">5:1</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+10%3A1&version=ESV">10:1</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A10&version=ESV">11:10</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A27&version=ESV">11:27</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+25%3A12&version=ESV">25:12</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+36%3A1&version=ESV">36:1</a>. What follows the word is usually a genealogy, or a review of how something already begun developed further.</p>

<p>The important point is that <em>toledot</em> nearly always opens an account that is <em>about to follow</em>. It does not close one that has just been given. This matters for <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+2%3A4&version=ESV">Genesis 2:4</a>. Critics who follow the Documentary Hypothesis assign 2:4a to the “P” source, which supposedly wrote the creation account of chapter 1.</p>

<p>But the usage of <em>toledot</em> elsewhere shows that 2:4a is better understood as the introduction to the story of Adam and Eve in Eden that fills the rest of chapter 2. A single author used one signpost consistently, and the signpost points forward.</p>

<h4>How the author handles the non-chosen lines</h4>

<p>Again and again the author deals first, and briefly, with the branches of the human family that are <em>not</em> part of the chosen line, and only then takes up the line that carries the promise:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Cain’s genealogy (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A17-24&version=ESV">4:17-24</a>) comes before Seth’s (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+4%3A25-26&version=ESV">4:25-26</a>).</li>
  <li>Japheth and Ham (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+10%3A1-8&version=ESV">10:1-8</a>) come before Shem (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+10%3A21-22&version=ESV">10:21-22</a>), even though Ham was probably the youngest of the three.</li>
  <li>Lot (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+19%3A29-30&version=ESV">19:29-30</a>) and Ishmael (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+25%3A12-15&version=ESV">25:12-15</a>) come before Isaac (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+25%3A19&version=ESV">25:19</a>).</li>
  <li>Esau’s descendants (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+36%3A1-10&version=ESV">36:1-10</a>) come before Jacob’s (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+37%3A2&version=ESV">37:2</a>).</li>
</ul>

<p>In each case the writer clears away the non-elect branches before turning to the patriarchs who had real faith in the LORD. Think of it as an editor clearing the table before serving the main course. That is the work of a planner with a single purpose, and it is hard to square with the theory that Genesis is a collection of unrelated sources awkwardly stitched together.</p>

<h3>7. Why This Matters</h3>

<p>If Genesis is a unified book written by Moses from older family records, then three things follow.</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Its history has roots.</strong> The stories were not invented centuries later; they rest on records kept close to the events.</li>
  <li><strong>Its message is coherent.</strong> The book has one theme, God’s covenant of grace, and every section serves it.</li>
  <li><strong>Its opening chapters deserve to be read as history.</strong> Not as myth, and not as a loose collection of legends, but as the first chapter of a real story that continues through the rest of Scripture.</li>
</ol>

<h3>8. Key Takeaways</h3>

<ul>
  <li><em>Genesis</em> means “beginning,” and the book is about the origins of the world, mankind, the nations and the covenant family.</li>
  <li>Tradition ascribes the book to Moses, and the New Testament, Exodus and Israel’s needs all support that tradition.</li>
  <li>Moses most likely worked as a Spirit-guided compiler of older family records.</li>
  <li>The five-part outline and the consistent use of <em>toledot</em> point to one author with one plan.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The title, the authorship tradition, the outline and the use of <em>toledot</em> all point the same way: Genesis is a unified book of origins, written to show how God called a people to Himself through covenant. If so, its opening chapters must be read as history with a message. In <a href="{{ gp2.url | relative_url }}">PT 2</a> we turn to Genesis 1 itself, and to how it stands against the pagan creation myths and the debate over the “days” of creation.</p>
