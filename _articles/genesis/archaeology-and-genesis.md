<h2>Introduction</h2>

<p>The Christian faith is a historical faith. It does not rest on a philosophy or a mood but on what God has done in time and space, and it invites examination.</p>

<p>That is why the question of archaeology and Genesis matters. If the opening chapters of the Bible were the late inventions of a people who knew nothing of the ancient world, the claim that the same Bible is God’s reliable word would be badly shaken. If they are set in a real world, told by a writer who knew that world well and who answered its stories with a clear and different vision of God, the case for Scripture is strengthened.</p>

<p>In 1996 Mario Seiglie published an article in <em>The Good News</em>, “Archaeology and Genesis: What Does the Record Show?”, which argued that excavation had answered the critics. His instinct was sound.</p>

<p>Some of his evidence is strong and deserves to be used with confidence. Some of it will not bear weight, and a wise defender of the faith removes weak supports before a critic knocks them out.</p>

<p>This study separates the two. Its conclusion is that Genesis can stand up to scrutiny, and that the Christian has no reason to fear what the ancient Near East has to say.</p>

<h2>Main Discussion</h2>

<p>Each part below builds on the one before it, so it is best read in order.</p>

<h3>1. Why History Matters to Christian Theology</h3>

<p>The Bible does not present itself as a collection of myths. Luke begins his Gospel by saying that he has carefully investigated everything from the beginning and wrote an orderly account so that Theophilus may “know the certainty of the things you have been taught” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+1%3A1-4&amp;version=ESV">Luke 1:1-4</a>).</p>

<p>Paul stakes the whole gospel on a historical event: “If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+15%3A17&amp;version=ESV">1 Corinthians 15:17</a>). The God of Scripture reveals himself by acting in history, and he expects his people to be able to say when and where.</p>

<p>Genesis belongs to that framework. Jesus himself treated the early chapters as history.</p>

<p>He grounded marriage in the creation of male and female and quoted <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A27&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 1:27</a> and 2:24 as the word of the Creator (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A4-6&amp;version=ESV">Matthew 19:4-6</a>), and he compared the coming of the Son of Man to “the days of Noah” before the flood (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+24%3A37-39&amp;version=ESV">Matthew 24:37-39</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+17%3A26-27&amp;version=ESV">Luke 17:26-27</a>). Luke’s genealogy traces his ancestry to “Adam, the son of God” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+3%3A38&amp;version=ESV">Luke 3:38</a>), and Paul builds his teaching on sin and redemption on the contrast between Adam and Christ (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+5%3A12-19&amp;version=ESV">Romans 5:12-19</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+15%3A21-22%2C+45&amp;version=ESV">1 Corinthians 15:21-22, 45</a>).</p>

<p>Hebrews and 2 Peter speak of Abel and Noah as real people (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+11%3A4%2C+7&amp;version=ESV">Hebrews 11:4, 7</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Peter+2%3A5&amp;version=ESV">2 Peter 2:5</a>). For the Christian, therefore, the historical character of Genesis is not a side issue. It is bound up with the authority of Christ and the apostles.</p>

<p>None of this means that the believer must wait for archaeology before he can trust Scripture. The believer trusts Scripture because Christ did, and because the Holy Spirit bears witness to the truth of God’s word. But the Bible invites testing, and the Christian can welcome what the spade finds as a help and not a threat, since God has written two books, the Scriptures and the created and historical world (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+19%3A1-4&amp;version=ESV">Psalm 19:1-4</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+1%3A20&amp;version=ESV">Romans 1:20</a>), and they do not contradict each other.</p>

<h3>2. What Archaeology Has Already Vindicated</h3>

<p>Before turning to Genesis, it is worth recalling how often the critics have had to revise their verdicts. Several biblical claims were once dismissed and have since been confirmed.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The Hittites.</strong> The Old Testament mentions the Hittites repeatedly (for example <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+23&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 23</a>), and in the nineteenth century some scholars said no such people existed. After excavation began at the Hittite capital Hattusa in 1906, the empire was recovered and thousands of its tablets were read.</li>
  <li><strong>The house of David.</strong> In 1993 and 1994 excavators at Tel Dan found fragments of a ninth-century BC Aramaic inscription that mentions the “house of David,” the earliest reference to David’s dynasty outside the Bible.</li>
  <li><strong>Pontius Pilate.</strong> In 1961 a stone found at Caesarea Maritima bore an inscription naming Pontius Pilate as prefect of Judea, confirming the Roman official named in all four Gospels.</li>
  <li><strong>Hezekiah’s tunnel.</strong> In 1880 a Hebrew inscription was found in the Siloam tunnel in Jerusalem, which records the cutting of a water tunnel in the way <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Kings+20%3A20&amp;version=ESV">2 Kings 20:20</a> describes.</li>
  <li><strong>Cyrus.</strong> The Cyrus Cylinder, found in 1879, records Cyrus’s policy of allowing exiled peoples to return home and rebuild their temples, which fits the decree in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezra+1&amp;version=ESV">Ezra 1</a>.</li>
</ul>

<p>These are not proofs of inspiration, and no honest scholar claims they are. But they establish a pattern: where the Bible speaks of people, places and events that can be checked, it has repeatedly turned out to be a reliable witness. That pattern gives the Christian good reason to give the same credit to the earliest chapters of Genesis, where the evidence is of a different kind.</p>

<h3>3. The Flood: A Memory That Genesis Explains Best</h3>

<p>The strongest part of Seiglie’s article concerns the flood, and it is the part a defender should press with confidence. The surviving Mesopotamian literature contains several flood accounts: the Sumerian <em>Eridu Genesis</em> (c. 1600 BC), the Akkadian <em>Atrahasis</em> epic (c. 1700 BC), and the eleventh tablet of the <em>Epic of Gilgamesh</em>, which George Smith of the British Museum announced in 1872 after reading it among the tablets from Nineveh.</p>

<p>Each tells of a warned hero, a boat, birds sent out and a sacrifice. The critic who once said that a universal flood story was a Hebrew invention must now explain why the oldest literate civilisation on earth remembered the same event centuries before the Hebrew Bible was complete.</p>

<p>The theological differences are what make Genesis stand out. In <em>Atrahasis</em> the gods send the flood because human noise disturbs their sleep, and in Gilgamesh they act without a clear reason and then quarrel over it.</p>

<p>In Genesis one holy God judges human violence and corruption (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6%3A5%2C+11-13&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 6:5, 11-13</a>) and saves a righteous man by grace. The Babylonian ship is a cube, which could not float in the manner of a vessel, whereas the ark of Genesis is given proportions suitable for riding out a flood (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6%3A15&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 6:15</a>).</p>

<p>The Old Testament scholar Gleason Archer drew from such contrasts the argument that the biblical account cannot be a reworking of the Babylonian, and many conservative scholars agree, while others hold that the writer of Genesis knew the tradition and retold it to correct it. In both views the result is the same: Genesis does not read like a myth that grew out of paganism but like a witness that stands against it.</p>

<p>The Mesopotamian traditions are also not the only ones. Flood stories appear on almost every continent, as the anthropologist James Frazer documented in his collection (1918) and as Alan Dundes’s edited volume shows (1988).</p>

<p>A defender may reasonably say that the widespread memory of a great flood fits what Genesis says, that all peoples descend from survivors of a single catastrophe (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+9%3A18-19&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 9:18-19</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+17%3A26&amp;version=ESV">Acts 17:26</a>). It should be said with care, since the stories also differ in many ways, but the point is a fair one.</p>

<h3>4. The Tower of Babel: A Genuine Tradition of Brick and Bitumen</h3>

<p>The story of Babel is the point where archaeology and Genesis come closest. The writer says that the builders used baked brick and bitumen in place of stone and mortar and aimed at a tower “with its top in the heavens” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A3-4&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 11:3-4</a>).</p>

<p>Those were the building materials of southern Mesopotamia, where stone is scarce, and the stepped temple tower called the ziggurat was the signature structure of its cities. A writer who was inventing a story far from Babylon would have had little reason to give such details correctly.</p>

<p>The best-known example was the Etemenanki, “the house of the foundation of heaven and earth,” in Babylon. Robert Koldewey excavated the city from 1899 to 1917 and found the square foundation of the tower, about 91 metres on each side.</p>

<p>The Esagila tablet, a cuneiform text now in the Louvre, describes a tower of seven stages with the same measurement, and the stele known as the Tower of Babel Stele shows Nebuchadnezzar II with a stepped tower. Herodotus saw the temple tower in the fifth century BC. Xerxes damaged it about 484 BC, and when Alexander tried to restore it in 331 BC he died before the work was done.</p>

<p>The very name Babylon, <em>bāb-ilim</em>, “gate of the god,” gives Genesis its irony: the city that called itself the gate of heaven was named by the Bible for confusion (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11%3A9&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 11:9</a>). Samuel Noah Kramer (1968) noted that a Sumerian poem about Enmerkar of Uruk speaks of a time when all peoples spoke one language, which is a striking echo, though scholars disagree about its reading.</p>

<h3>5. Arguments a Defender Should Retire</h3>

<p>A faithful defender cares more for the truth than for any particular argument, and some of the evidence in Seiglie’s article should be set aside. This is not a retreat. It protects the strong arguments from being tarred by the weak ones.</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>The Temptation Seal.</strong> The British Museum catalogues cylinder seal BM 89326 (c. 2250 BC) as a banquet scene, with a horned god, a seated woman, a date palm and a snake as ordinary elements of Akkadian art. It is not a picture of <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+3&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 3</a>, and George Smith’s early suggestion that it was has not been accepted. The second seal, from Tepe Gawra, is ambiguous and should not be offered as proof either. A critic who finds the museum entry will use it against everything else you say.</li>
  <li><strong>The “80,000 flood stories in 72 languages.”</strong> The figure cannot be traced to a primary source. Use Frazer and Dundes instead.</li>
  <li><strong>Woolley’s flood layer.</strong> Leonard Woolley announced in 1929 that silt at Ur was Noah’s flood, but similar layers at Kish and Shuruppak date to different periods, and archaeologists now read them as evidence of many local floods (Mallowan 1964). Genesis does not need a silt layer to stand.</li>
  <li><strong>The identity of the tower.</strong> The tower Koldewey found is a Nebuchadnezzar-era building on an older site, not the structure of <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+11&amp;version=ESV">Genesis 11</a>. It shows the kind of tower the writer knew, not that this tower was the one.</li>
</ol>

<p>A theologian will add that these are matters of method and not of faith. If an argument depends on a mistaken reading of an artifact, abandoning it costs the believer nothing, because the faith never rested on it.</p>

<h3>6. What Archaeology Cannot Do</h3>

<p>Archaeology can recover cities, tablets and buildings. It cannot show that God made the heavens and the earth, that he spoke to Adam, or that the ark came to rest on the mountains of Ararat.</p>

<p>Those claims rest on the testimony of Scripture, confirmed by the witness of Christ and the Spirit. A believer should neither expect the spade to prove them nor allow its silence to unsettle him. Absence of evidence in a few places is not evidence against, and the ancient world has preserved only a small part of what it once held.</p>

<p>What archaeology can do is remove excuses. It shows that the writer of Genesis was no stranger to the world he described, that his people shared the region with great civilisations whose records survive, and that his account is distinctive in the way a revelation would be: one Creator, a good creation, a moral judgment on human violence, and human beings made in the image of God. The Bible has never asked to be believed without being tested.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The record shows a Bible that can face the evidence. The Hittites, the house of David, Pilate and Cyrus have all appeared where the critics said they would not.</p>

<p>The flood stories of Mesopotamia and the ziggurats of Babylon confirm that Genesis is rooted in a real ancient setting and does not read like an echo of pagan myth. A wise defender will use these strong arguments, retire the weak ones, and rest his confidence where the Church has always rested it: on the God who spoke through the prophets and apostles and, in these last days, by his Son (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+1%3A1-2&amp;version=ESV">Hebrews 1:1-2</a>).</p>
