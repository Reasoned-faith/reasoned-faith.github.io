<h2>Introduction</h2>

<p>The Incarnation raises a famous puzzle. If Jesus is fully God, how can He also be fully human?</p>

<p>And if He is both, is He two people, or a strange mixture of the two? The Church’s answer fits into one sentence: <strong>Jesus Christ is one person with two natures.</strong> This article explains what that sentence means, why the Church formulated it, and how it helps us read the Bible’s hardest statements about Christ.</p>

<h2>Main Discussion</h2>

<p>Each part below builds on the one before it, so it is best read in order.</p>

<h3>1. Two Natures, Fully Preserved</h3>

<p>After the Incarnation, the Son of God has two natures: the divine nature He has always had, and a human nature He took on in the womb of the Virgin Mary. The Church insists that both are complete. Christ is not part God and part man, as if two halves had been joined. He has the whole divine nature and a whole human nature, including a human body, soul, mind, will and emotions.</p>

<p>The Council of Chalcedon (AD 451) expressed this with four short phrases that still shape Christian teaching. The two natures exist in Christ <em>without confusion</em>, <em>without change</em>, <em>without division</em> and <em>without separation</em>. These four phrases guard the truth on both sides.</p>

<h3>2. Why Not Half God and Half Man?</h3>

<p>The first two phrases, “without confusion” and “without change,” protect the difference between God and creatures. If the divine and human natures were blended into one new thing, Christ would be a hybrid, neither truly God nor truly man.</p>

<p>Such a being could not save us. So the two natures are not mixed. The divine nature stays divine, the human nature stays human, and each keeps its own integrity.</p>

<h3>3. Why Not Two Sons?</h3>

<p>The last two phrases, “without division” and “without separation,” protect the unity of Christ. If the two natures were kept so far apart that each belonged to a different person, there would be two Sons: an uncreated divine Son in heaven and a created human son on earth.</p>

<p>Scripture knows only one Son of God. For this reason some early Fathers spoke of “the God-Man.” They did not mean a composite being, but one person who has the whole divine nature and a whole human nature at the same time.</p>

<h3>4. What “Hypostatic Union” Means</h3>

<p>The technical name for this teaching is the <strong>hypostatic union</strong>. <em>Hypostasis</em> is a Greek word for an individual subject, the “who” behind the actions, and it is usually translated as <em>person</em>. The doctrine uses two different words for two different questions:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Nature</strong> answers the question “<em>what</em> is He?” Christ is God, and Christ is man.</li>
  <li><strong>Person (hypostasis)</strong> answers the question “<em>who</em> is He?” Christ is the eternal Son, one single subject.</li>
</ul>

<p>The two natures are united because one and the same person governs both. The human Jesus and the divine Son are not two persons but one. For this reason the man Jesus never speaks to the Son as to someone else. He cannot say “you” to the Second Divine Person, because they are one and the same “I.”</p>

<p>Christ’s humanity does not have a separate human “personal core” beside the divine one. Instead, the eternal Son, who is already the personal subject of the divine nature, also became the personal subject of the human nature He took on. Nothing human is missing. The human nature is complete, and it belongs to the Son.</p>

<h3>5. Reading the Hard Sayings of Jesus</h3>

<p>The doctrine of the two natures is more than a theory. It helps us read passages that seem to contradict each other. In the Gospel of John, Jesus says two things that appear to pull in opposite directions:</p>

<blockquote>
  “My Father is greater than I.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2014%3A28&version=KJV">John 14:28 (KJV)</a></cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote>
  “I and my Father are one.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2010%3A30&version=KJV">John 10:30 (KJV)</a></cite>
</blockquote>

<p>The Church’s rule for reading statements like these is to ask which nature they refer to. As a true man, who lived in obedience and dependence, Jesus could truly say that the Father is greater. As true God, who shares one divine nature with the Father, He could just as truly say that the two are one. The same person speaks both sentences, and each is true in its own respect.</p>

<p>Many passages that are used to argue that Christ was only a man, or that He was less than the Father, are best understood this way. They are real testimonies to His humanity, and they do not deny His divinity.</p>

<h3>6. Can We Say “God Died”?</h3>

<p>Because the two natures belong to one person, we can say things about the person that are true of only one nature. We can say “God came into our world” and “God suffered and died on the cross.” These statements are true because the one who died is the Son, who is God.</p>

<p>They must be understood carefully, however. In His divine nature the Son cannot suffer or die, since God’s nature does not change or perish.</p>

<p>The Son suffered and died <em>in His human nature</em>. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) put it in a balanced way: according to His divinity He is immortal and unable to suffer, while according to His humanity He became able to suffer and die.</p>

<p>This careful balance guards against two opposite errors:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Patripassianism.</strong> The view of the early Modalists, who said that the Father Himself suffered, because they did not distinguish the persons of the Trinity.</li>
  <li><strong>Theopaschitism, in its wrong form.</strong> The claim that Christ suffered even in His divine nature, so that “God as God” can suffer. Some later thinkers have taken this path, often because they read the human-sounding language about God in Scripture too literally.</li>
</ul>

<p>The word also has a correct meaning. It is true to say that God suffered <em>in Christ’s humanity</em>, because that humanity is united to the Son’s divine person. This is what the Church affirms when she says that the Son suffered for us on the cross.</p>

<h3>7. Two Formulas to Remember</h3>

<p>The Trinity and the Incarnation can be summarized in two short statements:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The Trinity:</strong> three persons (Father, Son and Holy Spirit), one essence or nature (the Godhead).</li>
  <li><strong>The Incarnation:</strong> one person (the Son), two natures (divinity and humanity).</li>
</ul>

<p>The first formula explains <em>who</em> came to redeem us. The second explains <em>how</em> He came. Taken together, they give the full picture of Christ’s identity.</p>

<h3>8. A Union That Does Not End</h3>

<p>The hypostatic union was not a temporary arrangement for the years of Jesus’ earthly life. Once the Son took on a human nature, He kept it. He rose in a real body, ascended in it (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%201%3A9-11&version=KJV">Acts 1:9-11</a>), and remains true God and true man forever. The risen Christ still carries our humanity into the presence of the Father.</p>

<h3>9. How This Teaching Developed</h3>

<p>The Church did not invent this doctrine in the fifth century. It grew out of the New Testament and the earliest Christian writers.</p>

<p>Ignatius of Antioch, writing in the early second century, already speaks of Christ as both fully human and fully divine, and Tertullian, in the early third century, described one person with two substances. The Council of Ephesus (AD 431) affirmed the unity of Christ as one person, and the Council of Chalcedon (AD 451) gave the classic statement of the two natures.</p>

<p>Later councils clarified details of Christ’s humanity. The Council of Vienne (1312), for example, taught that the Son took in the womb of the Virgin a human body capable of suffering and a rational soul. This confirms that Christ’s humanity was complete, and not just a body moved by God from the inside.</p>

<h3>10. Answering a Common Objection</h3>

<p><strong>“How can one person be infinite and finite, or immortal and mortal?”</strong></p>

<p>The objection assumes that the two natures are mixed together, so that the same thing is both infinite and finite in the same way. The doctrine says the opposite. The divine nature has divine properties, and the human nature has human properties. They are not blended, so there is no contradiction. A single person can truly act and be described through either nature.</p>

<p>An imperfect comparison may help. Every human being is one person who has both a physical body and a spiritual soul.</p>

<p>We say “I am hungry” and “I am thinking” without feeling that we are two people. The comparison does not explain the mystery, since God and creation are not two parts of one being, but it shows that having two very different kinds of reality in one person is not meaningless.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The teaching that Christ is one person with two natures does not try to make the mystery simple. It tries to protect it from distortion.</p>

<p>It keeps Jesus fully God, so that His death has infinite worth. It keeps Him fully human, so that He can truly stand in our place. And it keeps Him one, so that the Son who was with the Father in the beginning is the same person who was born in Bethlehem and died on Calvary.</p>

<blockquote>
  “And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%201%3A14&version=KJV">John 1:14 (KJV)</a></cite>
</blockquote>
