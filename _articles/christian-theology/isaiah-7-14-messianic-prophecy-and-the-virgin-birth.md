<h2>Introduction</h2>

<p>Isaiah 7:14 is one of the most debated verses in the Bible. Matthew applies it to the birth of Jesus (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%201%3A22-23&version=KJV">Matthew 1:22-23</a>). Yet many readers ask two questions. First, was the prophecy about the Messiah at all, or only about a child born in Isaiah’s own day? Second, does the Hebrew word in the verse really mean “virgin”?</p>

<blockquote>
  “Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign; Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%207%3A14&version=KJV">Isaiah 7:14 (KJV)</a></cite>
</blockquote>

<p>This article summarizes and explains an essay on these two questions. What makes the essay unusual is that it leans heavily on Jewish sources: the ancient Aramaic translations of the Old Testament called targums, the Talmud, and several well-known modern Jewish scholars. The essay argues that the findings of these scholars, honestly reported, support the Christian reading.</p>

<p>We will take its two questions in order and finish with the objections it answers. One note on numbering: the essay cites verses as in Hebrew Bibles, so “Isaiah 9:5–6” there is Isaiah 9:6–7 in most English Bibles. The links below use the English numbering.</p>

<h2>Main Discussion</h2>

<p>Each part below builds on the one before it, so it is best read in order.</p>

<h3>1. Why the Ancient Targums Matter</h3>

<p>A targum is an ancient Jewish translation of the Old Testament into Aramaic, the everyday language of many Jews after the exile. Targums were not word-for-word. They paraphrased and added explanation, which means they show how Jewish readers understood the text.</p>

<p>Two targums matter most here:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Targum Jonathan</strong> is the official targum to the Prophets. It contains many messianic readings of Isaiah and Micah.</li>
  <li><strong>Targum Onkelos</strong> is the official targum to the Torah. It treats the promises of <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%203%3A15&version=KJV">Genesis 3:15</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2049%3A10&version=KJV">Genesis 49:10</a> as messianic.</li>
</ul>

<p>If the targums reflect what Jews believed before Christianity spread, they are valuable witnesses. Their readings could not have been shaped by seeing the prophecies fulfilled in Christ.</p>

<h3>2. When Were the Targums Written?</h3>

<p>Scholars disagree about the dates, and the essay presents several views:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Samson Levey</strong> argues that the official targums likely come from the second century BC. He notes that they avoid the full title “King Messiah”, which could have sounded like treason under the Hasmonean rulers.</li>
  <li><strong>Menahem Kasher</strong> traces Onkelos and related targums back to the time of Ezra, in the fifth century BC. He connects this with <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Nehemiah%208%3A7-8&version=KJV">Nehemiah 8:7-8</a>, where the Levites gave the sense of the law so that the people understood it.</li>
  <li><strong>Jacob Neusner</strong>, a leading Jewish scholar, holds that the targums contain ideas from a time before their final editing.</li>
  <li><strong>Bruce Chilton</strong> points to the Isaiah Targum on <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2025%3A2&version=KJV">Isaiah 25:2</a>, which says that the nations will never build a temple in Jerusalem. That statement makes sense only before AD 136, when a temple to Jupiter was dedicated there. He also argues that much targum material was already in oral use in the time of Jesus.</li>
</ul>

<p>The debate continues. The essay’s main point does not depend on settling it. Whatever their final date, the targums reflect ancient Jewish understanding of the messianic prophecies, formed without “hindsight” from Christian fulfillment. If some parts are older than others, the prophecies are the likeliest to be old.</p>

<p>The essay also notes an admission. Neusner, Levey and the scholar H. J. Schoeps all grant that some targums were deliberately adjusted to counter Christian use of the prophecies. We will return to this.</p>

<h3>3. New Evidence: The Early Rabbis’ Silence About the Messiah</h3>

<p>The essay adds an argument from silence, based on Neusner’s survey of what the rabbis wrote about the Messiah after Jerusalem fell in AD 70:</p>

<ul>
  <li>The <strong>Mishnah</strong> (about AD 200) hardly mentions a messianic figure. Neusner suspects the reason is deep disappointment after the failed Bar Kokhba revolt.</li>
  <li>The <strong>Tosefta</strong> shows little concern with the Messiah.</li>
  <li>The <strong>Jerusalem Talmud</strong> does not raise messianic questions, even where a passage of the Mishnah would naturally invite them.</li>
  <li>Rabbinic commentaries such as <strong>Genesis Rabbah</strong>, <strong>Sifra</strong> and the <strong>Sifre</strong> give the subject little weight.</li>
  <li>The <strong>Babylonian Talmud</strong> (completed around AD 500–600) shows more interest, but its points are few: a time of tribulation comes first, people should not calculate when the Messiah will come, history has three parts, he will come to a worthy generation, and he will be from the house of David.</li>
</ul>

<p>Even in the later Talmud, the great messianic prophecies of Isaiah are not used. The essay concludes that the rich messianic readings of the Isaiah Targum do not fit this later climate and probably come from an earlier time.</p>

<p>It adds two small supporting details:</p>

<ul>
  <li>The Aramaic word <em>memra</em>, often used in the targums as a way of speaking about God, does not appear in the later Jewish literature Neusner surveyed, according to the essay’s reading of Jastrow’s dictionary.</li>
  <li>At <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%205%3A16&version=KJV">Isaiah 5:16</a> the Isaiah Targum calls God “the Holy One” without the phrase “Blessed be He”, which later rabbis added consistently. That points to an early origin for the line.</li>
</ul>

<p>On this basis the essay treats the targums as a fair guide to early Jewish understanding.</p>

<h3>4. Is Isaiah 7:14 About the Messiah?</h3>

<p>Scholars have offered three main answers. Some say the child was Hezekiah, the son of King Ahaz. Others say it is Christ. A third group says both, because a prophecy can have more than one fulfillment.</p>

<h4>The child as a sign</h4>

<p>The verse calls the birth a sign. Some scholars say an ordinary birth could still be a sign. Otto Kaiser holds that even ordinary events can be called signs in the Old Testament, and J. H. Hayes and S. A. Irvine go further, saying the words need not announce a sign at all.</p>

<p>The essay finds these claims weak, for four reasons:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The offer was enormous.</strong> Isaiah had told Ahaz to ask for any sign, “as deep as Sheol or as high as heaven” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%207%3A10-12&version=KJV">Isaiah 7:10-12</a>). To answer with the birth of an ordinary royal child would be, in the essay’s words, like the old saying of mountains in labor bringing forth a mouse.</li>
  <li><strong>Ahaz already had a son.</strong> He had sacrificed a son by fire (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%2016%3A3&version=KJV">2 Kings 16:3</a>), and the essay reasons that this son was born before Hezekiah. So the promise of a son to continue the line would add little.</li>
  <li><strong>The line was about to be cut down.</strong> Isaiah foresaw the house of David reduced to a stump, from which a shoot would later grow (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2011%3A1&version=KJV">Isaiah 11:1</a>).</li>
  <li><strong>The Hebrew says “the” young woman.</strong> The article suggests a specific woman, not just any wife of the king.</li>
</ul>

<h4>The link with Isaiah 9</h4>

<p>Even scholars who do not see Christ in the passage generally agree that the child of Isaiah 9 is the same child as in Isaiah 7. The titles given to that child are too grand for an ordinary king: <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%209%3A6-7&version=KJV">“Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace”</a>.</p>

<p>Some respond that these are just throne names given to kings. The essay replies that no other king in the Old Testament is called “Mighty God”. That Hebrew title occurs only five times, and each time it means God himself: here and in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2010%3A21&version=KJV">Isaiah 10:21</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%2010%3A17&version=KJV">Deuteronomy 10:17</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah%2032%3A18&version=KJV">Jeremiah 32:18</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Nehemiah%209%3A32&version=KJV">Nehemiah 9:32</a>. The essay also calls strained the suggestion by Hayes and Irvine that the child is Ahaz himself.</p>

<h3>5. What the Jewish Sources Reveal</h3>

<p>The essay’s most distinctive claim is that Jewish sources themselves point toward a messianic reading, and that later Jews moved away from it.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Hillel</strong>, the great teacher at the time of Christ, is reported in the Talmud (Sanhedrin 99a) to have said that Israel would have no Messiah, since they already had him in the days of Hezekiah.</li>
  <li><strong>Johanan ben Zakkai</strong>, according to the Talmud (Berakhot 28b), said: “Prepare a throne for Hezekiah, king of Judah, who is coming.” Levey calls this especially significant, because Johanan spoke after the destruction of the Temple in AD 70.</li>
  <li><strong>Levey</strong> also notes that the tenses of the Targum on Isaiah 9:5 suggest that its writer had Hezekiah in mind as the Messiah. This, the essay says, hints at an early date, since the Jews later dropped that idea. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 99a) cites Rabbi Joseph arguing that it could not be Hezekiah, because <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah%209%3A9&version=KJV">Zechariah 9:9</a>, written after Hezekiah’s time, still promises a Messiah to come.</li>
  <li><strong>Neusner</strong> observes that once Christians began to say the Messiah had come, Jews had reason to reject the claim that Hezekiah had been the Messiah.</li>
</ul>

<p>The essay then builds its case in steps. The Targum Jonathan calls Isaiah 9:5–6 messianic. Scholars commonly agree that the child there is the same child as in 7:14. So 7:14 must be messianic as well. The Targum does not label 7:14 messianic, but the essay explains this by the deliberate changes that Jewish writers made to keep Christians from using the prophecies.</p>

<p>Several Jewish scholars admit such changes. Levey quotes with qualified approval the view of J. Bowker that targum interpretations were often designed to exclude the Christian argument, though he adds that Bowker is too general and too certain. Schoeps says that reports about the atoning value of rabbis’ martyrdoms were carefully worded to avoid helping Christians. He adds that atoning power was credited to the death of Moses to remove the reference of Isaiah 53 to Christ.</p>

<h3>6. One Prophecy, More Than One Fulfillment?</h3>

<p>The essay also suggests that Isaiah 7:14 could refer to both Hezekiah and Christ. Augustine recognized in <em>The City of God</em> (Book 17, chapter 3) that some Old Testament prophecies refer only to Old Testament people or events, some only to Christ and the Church, and some to both. He noticed this by seeing that the prophecy fits partly one and partly the other. Some details in Isaiah 7 seem to fit Hezekiah better, and some fit Christ better.</p>

<p>The essay then turns to the Second Vatican Council. In its teaching on Mary, the Council says that these ancient texts, read in the Church and in the light of later revelation, bring out more clearly the figure of the Mother of the Redeemer. It names the promise of <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%203%3A15&version=KJV">Genesis 3:15</a> and adds:</p>

<blockquote>
  “Similarly she is the Virgin who will conceive and bear a Son whose name will be called Emmanuel.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_en.html">Lumen Gentium, no. 55</a>, citing Isaiah 7:14, Micah 5:2–3 and Matthew 1:22–23</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>Behind this is the principle that the chief author of Scripture is the Holy Spirit, who can intend more than the human author saw at the time of writing. The essay draws two conclusions. The full sense of Isaiah 7:14 was probably not clear at the start, even to Isaiah. But it has become clear with time and with the Spirit’s guidance, and so the Holy Spirit did intend the messianic sense.</p>

<h3>7. Does Isaiah 7:14 Speak of a Virgin Birth?</h3>

<p>The essay now turns to its second question. It looks at the Hebrew word <em>almah</em> and at the early Greek translation, the Septuagint (LXX).</p>

<p>The essay grants that <em>almah</em> does not strictly mean “virgin”. It means a young woman of marriageable age, who would be presumed to be a virgin. The word appears only seven times in the Old Testament, and the LXX translators rendered it with the Greek word for “virgin” (<em>parthenos</em>) in just two of them:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2024%3A43&version=KJV">Genesis 24:43</a></strong>: the young woman who comes to draw water is the one Isaac’s servant hopes to find as a bride. The LXX says “virgin”.</li>
  <li><strong><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%207%3A14&version=KJV">Isaiah 7:14</a></strong>: the LXX again says “virgin”.</li>
  <li><strong><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%202%3A8&version=KJV">Exodus 2:8</a></strong>: the sister of the infant Moses. She was probably a virgin, but the LXX uses a broader word for a young woman.</li>
  <li><strong><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Proverbs%2030%3A19&version=KJV">Proverbs 30:19</a></strong>: “the way of a man with a maid”. The LXX changes the sense and reads “in his youth”.</li>
  <li><strong><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2068%3A25&version=KJV">Psalm 68:26</a></strong> (68:25 in English Bibles): young women playing timbrels. The LXX again uses the broader word.</li>
  <li><strong><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Song%20of%20Solomon%201%3A3&version=KJV">Song of Solomon 1:3</a></strong> and <strong><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Song%20of%20Solomon%206%3A8&version=KJV">Song of Solomon 6:8</a></strong>: the LXX again avoids “virgin”. The essay argues that in 6:8 a girl who is neither a queen nor a concubine is likely a virgin.</li>
</ul>

<p>The essay concludes that the Septuagint translators were extremely careful with the word “virgin”. They used it only twice, and one of those two times is Isaiah 7:14. So the translators were evidently convinced that the word meant a virgin there.</p>

<h3>8. Objections Answered</h3>

<h4>René Laurentin’s objections</h4>

<p>The French theologian René Laurentin raised two main objections. The essay replies to each:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>“The LXX changed who names the child.”</strong> In the Hebrew, the mother gives the name. In the LXX, the prophet says to Ahaz, “you will call”. Laurentin argued that this weakens the Greek witness. The essay answers that the Old Testament text was not yet firmly fixed when the LXX was made, so the LXX probably followed a different textual tradition. The Isaiah scroll found at Qumran reads “one will call”, and the Targum Jonathan reads “she will call”. Mothers also gave names in the Old Testament even when a human father was present, as in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%204%3A1&version=KJV">Genesis 4:1</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%204%3A25&version=KJV">Genesis 4:25</a>, <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2019%3A36&version=KJV">Genesis 19:36</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2029%3A32&version=KJV">Genesis 29:32</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>“The LXX is loose with <em>parthenos</em>, as with Dinah.”</strong> Laurentin pointed to Dinah, who is called a virgin after her violation in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2034&version=KJV">Genesis 34:3-4</a>. The essay says this was a slip: the Hebrew has a plain word for “young woman”, not “virgin”. It suggests the LXX translators understood the narrative as repeating its story in a concentric pattern, and so took the word to refer to Dinah before the violation. In <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2024&version=KJV">Genesis 24:14</a>, where the context clearly shows the girl is a virgin, the LXX is actually more precise than the Hebrew.</li>
</ul>

<h4>Otto Kaiser’s objection</h4>

<p>Kaiser says that, according to the lexicons, <em>parthenos</em> does not necessarily mean virginity in the strict sense. The essay replies that the Septuagint’s own usage matters more than pagan Greek usage, and that the pagan examples are few. It then checks them:</p>

<ul>
  <li>In Homer’s <em>Iliad</em> (2:511–514) a “maiden” is visited secretly by the god Ares. She was honorable and would have been thought a virgin before he came.</li>
  <li>In Pindar (<em>Pythian</em> 3:31–34) a girl is called unwedded after a fall from virtue. She would popularly have been considered a virgin.</li>
  <li>In Aristophanes (<em>Clouds</em> 530–531) a woman says she was still a virgin when she exposed her child, to hide what she had done.</li>
  <li>In Sophocles (<em>Trachiniae</em> 1219–1220) Iole is called a maiden, though Heracles says she had lain at his side.</li>
</ul>

<p>The essay draws three conclusions. All the examples are from poets, who use words freely. In each case the woman would popularly have been considered a virgin. And the examples are few, so they do not represent the general Greek usage.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The essay draws five conclusions:</p>

<ol>
  <li>New evidence, chiefly the early rabbis’ lack of interest in the great prophecies, suggests that the Targums Jonathan and Onkelos are early, probably no later than AD 200 and perhaps much earlier. At the least, Targum Jonathan reflects Jewish understanding of the prophecies without the help of seeing them fulfilled in Christ.</li>
  <li>Targum Jonathan clearly treats Isaiah 9:5–6 as messianic. Since 7:14 speaks of the same child in the same section of Isaiah, 7:14 should be messianic too. The Targum’s silence on 7:14 is explained by the changes Jews later made to keep Christians from using these texts, a fact admitted by several modern Jewish scholars.</li>
  <li>The Second Vatican Council teaches that 7:14, read in the light of later revelation, speaks of the Messiah and of Mary his Mother in a virginal conception. The Holy Spirit intended this even if Isaiah did not see it fully.</li>
  <li>For those who accept the teaching of the Council, both points are clear: Isaiah 7:14 is messianic, and it speaks of a virginal conception.</li>
  <li>A careful look at the Hebrew and the Septuagint shows that <em>almah</em> need not mean virgin, but that it usually does, and the Septuagint understood it that way in Isaiah 7:14.</li>
</ol>

<p>This remains a debated area. Jewish scholars and many Christian scholars read Isaiah 7:14 differently, and the dating of the targums is still disputed. A fair reader should weigh this argument against the best case on the other side, and then judge it by the evidence.</p>
