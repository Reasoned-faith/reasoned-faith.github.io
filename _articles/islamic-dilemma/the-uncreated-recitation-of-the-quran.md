<h2>Introduction</h2>

<p>The references in this post are taken from <em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, written by Abu ‘Abdir-Rahman ‘Abdullah ibn Al-Imam Ahmad (d. 290H), translated by Abu Hajar and published by Al-Aqeedah in 2022. All emphasis will be mine.</p>

<p>Since the Quran is supposed to be Allah’s Word, Muslims must therefore deny that it is created. Nor are they permitted to say that their own recitation is created. As if this weren’t amazing enough, Muslims are even forbidden from asserting that the Quran is not Allah!</p>

<p>This article sets out what these early Sunni reports actually say, explains the arguments behind them, and then looks at the part that is hardest to defend: the reports in which respected scholars and rulers demand that those who disagree be put to death. Along the way it explains the technical terms, places the scholars on a timeline, and describes how Sunni Muslims respond today. A glossary and a list of further reading are given at the end.</p>

<h2>Main Discussion</h2>

<p>Each part below builds on the one before it, so it is best read in order.</p>

<h3>1. Who Is Speaking, and What Was the Dispute?</h3>

<p><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em> is a collection of early reports on the creed of the “people of the Sunnah.” Its author, ‘Abdullah, was the son of Ahmad ibn Hanbal, the famous jurist and hadith scholar who became the symbol of resistance during the <em>mihna</em>, the “inquisition” of the early ninth century. During that period several Abbasid caliphs pressed scholars to confess that the Quran was created.</p>

<p>Ahmad refused, was imprisoned and was flogged, and afterwards the position that he defended became the standard Sunni one. The book therefore shows us the creed of the winning side, in the words of its own supporters.</p>

<p>The question itself can be stated simply. The Quran calls itself the “Word of Allah.” Is that Word something Allah <em>made</em>, like the heavens and the earth, or is it an eternal attribute of Allah, like His knowledge?</p>

<p>The side that said it was made (chiefly the Mu‘tazilites, with the caliph al-Ma’mun as their royal patron) argued that only Allah is eternal, so anything else, including a book that exists in time and was revealed in Arabic, must have been created. The side that said it was not made (the traditionists around Ahmad) argued that speech is an attribute of the speaker, and that to call the speech created is to say that Allah once lacked it or ceased to speak.</p>

<p>Today the uncreated view is the accepted Sunni position, while Shia Muslims hold that the Quran is created. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quranic_createdness">Wikipedia’s article on Quranic createdness</a> gives a good overview of both sides.</p>

<p>Three labels appear again and again in the reports, and it helps to know them in advance:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The Jahmiyyah:</strong> the followers of Jahm ibn Safwan, and later a general insult for anyone who said that the Quran is created. Bishr Al-Marisi is named as one of their leaders.</li>
  <li><strong>The Waqifah:</strong> those who say that the Quran is the words of Allah but refuse to say whether it is created or not.</li>
  <li><strong>The Lafidhiyyah:</strong> those who say that their pronunciation (<em>lafz</em>) of the Quran is created.</li>
</ul>

<p>So there were not two positions but at least four, and the reports condemn every one of them except the strictest.</p>

<p>According to <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihna">Wikipedia’s article on the Mihna</a>, al-Ma’mun proclaimed the doctrine in 827, and the formal test followed six years later. <a href="https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/mihna">Encyclopedia.com’s entry on the Mihna</a> adds that modern scholars still debate why al-Ma’mun pressed the matter, and that there is no consensus. Many see it as an attempt to bring the scholars under caliphal authority rather than a purely theological project.</p>

<h3>2. The Basic Claim: The Quran Is the Word of Allah and Is Not Created</h3>

<p>The first group of reports states the creed in simple terms and attaches the penalty of unbelief to the opposite view.</p>

<blockquote>
  “79. I heard my father when ‘Abdullah ibn ‘Umar – known by Mushakanadah – asked him about the Quran? So he said: ‘It is the Word of Allah and it not created.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 79</cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote>
  “81. … I heard my father … say: ‘The Quran is the Word of Allah and not created. And whoever says: (It is) created, <strong>he is a kafir</strong>.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 81</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>The same report places Ibn Mas‘ud and the Prophet himself behind the idea. The Quran is described as something one can use to measure one’s love for Allah, and its rank above all other speech is compared to Allah’s rank above all creation:</p>

<blockquote>
  “125. … ‘Abdullah ibn Mas’ud … said: ‘Whoever loves to know that he loves Allah then let him present himself to the Quran. Then if he loves the Quran, he loves Allah. Because verily, the Quran is the Word of Allah.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 125</cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote>
  “128. … The Messenger of Allah said: ‘Allah says: Whoever is occupied from My remembrance and asking Me due to reciting the Quran, I will give him the best reward of those who ask. And <strong>the superiority of the Quran over all speech, is like the superiority of Allah over His creation</strong>.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 128</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>Notice the comparison in no. 128. Allah is not created, and the Quran’s relationship to other speech is likened to His relationship to creation. That is already a very high place for a book to occupy.</p>

<p>It is worth being precise about what these two reports do and do not say. Report 125 says only that the Quran is Allah’s Word, which no Muslim denies, and report 128 is a comparison of rank. Neither says in so many words that the Quran is <em>uncreated</em>. The step from “Allah’s Word” to “uncreated” is made by the scholars themselves, by inference, and the arguments below show how they made it.</p>

<h3>3. The Arguments Used to Prove It</h3>

<p>The reports do not only assert the doctrine. They also give the reasoning that the early scholars used, and the quality of that reasoning is worth examining.</p>

<h4>1. “Is Allah created?”</h4>

<p>Several scholars simply move from the divine names or from a verse where Allah speaks to the conclusion that the Quran is uncreated:</p>

<blockquote>
  “156. … I asked ‘Abdullah ibn Dawud about the Quran, so he said: ‘Al-‘Aziz Al-Jabbar Al-Mutakabbir, is this created?’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 156</cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote>
  “157. … Yahya ibn Sa’id said to me: ‘What will they do with: “Qul HowAllahu Ahad.” (And) what will they do with this verse? “Verily I am Allah.” (Al-Qasas 28:30) Is this created?’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 157</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>The argument runs like this. <a href="https://quran.com/28/30">Quran 28:30</a> records Allah speaking to Moses and saying, “Verily I am Allah.” If those words were created, then a created thing would be saying “I am Allah.” The same reasoning is applied to <a href="https://quran.com/1/5">1:5</a> in the next report, where the reciter asks whether the words “You (alone) we worship and You we ask for help” can be created:</p>

<blockquote>
  “167. … I heard Hasan ibn Musa Al-Ashyab say: ‘I seek refuge with the Hearing and Knowledgeable from the accursed Shaytan. In the Name of Allah the Most Merciful the Most Beneficent.’ ‘You (alone) we worship and You we ask for help.’ (Al-Fatihah 1:5) Is this created?”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 167</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>There is a weakness here that the reports never address. In Quran 1:5 it is the worshipper who is speaking, not Allah. Taken seriously, the argument would make the human words in the Quran uncreated as well, which is why the later doctrine has to speak of a “recitation” that is somehow both human and divine.</p>

<p>The Mu‘tazilite reply to the 28:30 argument was that the words spoken at the burning bush were a created utterance that Allah brought into being so that Moses could hear it, in the way that a messenger may say “I am the king” while delivering the king’s message. The early scholars do not engage with that reply. They only ask whether the words can be created, as if the question answered itself.</p>

<h4>2. Allah’s speech is like Allah’s look</h4>

<blockquote>
  “166. … Sulayman ibn Harb … said: ‘The Quran is not created.’ So I said to him: ‘You never used to say this, so what happened to you?’ He said: ‘I derived it from the Book of Allah: “And Allah will not speak to them nor will He look at them.” (<a href="https://quran.com/3/77">Alu ‘Imran 3:77</a>) So the Speech and the Look <strong>are the same</strong>.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 166</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>The translator’s footnote explains that this means that just as the Look of Allah is not created, the Speech and Word of Allah are also not created. The verse, however, only places speaking and looking in the same sentence. It does not say that they are the same kind of thing, and the argument could equally be used to prove that Allah’s looking is a book.</p>

<h4>3. “Did He create it before He spoke it or after?”</h4>

<blockquote>
  “… I said to Isma’il ibn Hammad ibn Abi Hanifah – and he (was among those who) said: ‘The Quran is created.’ So I said to him: ‘Did He create it before He spoke it or after He spoke it?’ He said: ‘So he became quiet.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 160 and following</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>This is a dilemma and not an argument from Scripture. If Allah created the Quran before speaking it, then there was something that He had not yet said. If He created it after speaking it, then His speech and the Quran are two different things, which is exactly what the Jahmiyyah claimed. The report records that the man had no answer.</p>

<p>The silence of one man in a recorded exchange proves little, since a person can be stumped by a question without the question being unanswerable. The Mu‘tazilites in fact had an answer: they held that Allah’s speaking <em>is</em> the act of creating the speech, so there is no gap between the two. The dilemma only works if one has already assumed that speech must be an eternal attribute and cannot be an act.</p>

<h4>4. Seeking refuge in the Quran</h4>

<blockquote>
  “172. … I entered upon a man to seek refuge (with Allah) for him for a pain he had. So he said: ‘The Quran is not created. And that is because everyone who seeks refuge for me says: “I seek refuge with Allah for you. I seek refuge with the Quran for you.” So I knew that the Quran is not created.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 172</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>This is a striking piece of reasoning. People ask Allah to protect them and they also ask the Quran to protect them. Since one can only seek refuge with a Creator and never with a creature, the Quran must be uncreated. A practice among ordinary believers is being used to establish a doctrine of the Godhead.</p>

<p>Sunnis who defend the argument point to the hadith in which the Prophet says, “I seek refuge in the perfect words of Allah from the evil of what He created,” which is recorded in <em>Sahih Muslim</em>. The reasoning is that one seeks refuge in Allah’s words but not in anything created, so the words cannot be among the created things. The weakness is that the hadith speaks of “words” (<em>kalimat</em>) in the plural, which Muslims do not usually take to mean the Quran specifically, and the argument assumes in advance that seeking refuge is something only permitted with a Creator.</p>

<h4>5. “It is neither Allah nor other than Allah”</h4>

<p>The most revealing report is the long answer attributed to Abu ‘Ubayd Al-Qasim ibn Sallam. The question that the Jahmiyyah put to the Sunnis was simple: is the Quran Allah, or is it something other than Allah?</p>

<blockquote>
  “177. … ‘If the jahmi says to you: “Inform me about the Quran, is it Allah or is it something other than Allah?” then the answer is that it is said to him: “You have asked an impossible question.” … Allah said: “The revelation of a Book in which there is no doubt, from the Lord of all the worlds.” (<a href="https://quran.com/32/2">Al-Sajdah 32:2</a>) So it is from Allah and He did not say: ‘It is Me,’ <strong>nor ‘it is other than Me.’</strong> He verily named it His Word. So for us there is nothing (to say) about it except what he adorned it with, and we negate for it what he negated.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 177</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>This deserves careful attention. Abu ‘Ubayd refuses both answers.</p>

<p>The Quran is not Allah, but it is also not “other than” Allah. Everything that exists is either Allah or something other than Allah, and the question is therefore not “impossible” but exhaustive.</p>

<p>If the Quran is not Allah, it is something other than Allah, and in Islamic theology something other than Allah is created. The answer works only by declining to apply the category.</p>

<p>The Sunni defence is that this formula is meant to express how an attribute relates to its owner. Allah’s knowledge, for instance, is not identical to Allah, yet it cannot be separated from Him or described as a second being.</p>

<p>Muslim theologians used the phrase “neither He nor other than He” of the divine attributes in general. The difficulty is that the Quran is a book that exists in print and in sound, and a book is not an attribute in the way that knowledge is. This is the very pressure that later led the Ash‘aris and Maturidis to say that only the <em>meaning</em> is uncreated (see below).</p>

<p>He then deals with an objection from <a href="https://quran.com/16/40">Quran 16:40</a>, “Verily Our Word to something when We want it (to occur or be) is that We say to it ‘Be’, and then it is.” The Jahmiyyah argued that the Quran is a “something,” and therefore the Word “Be” created it. Abu ‘Ubayd replies that the saying came before the something, so Allah’s Word cannot be one of the things it brings into existence. This is the standard argument that if Allah’s Word were itself created, it would need another “Be” to bring it into being, and so on without end.</p>

<p>Notice what has happened. A book that Muslims can hold, print and recite has been placed on the Creator’s side of the line between Creator and creation, and the reasoning that does this is that one may not say what it is. That is the heart of the dilemma.</p>

<h3>4. Even the Recitation Is Protected</h3>

<p>A more extraordinary step follows. It is not only the Quran “in itself” that must not be called created. Even the reciter’s own act is placed under the ban.</p>

<blockquote>
  Ahmad was asked about “a man who says: ‘The recitation (of the Quran) is created and our words are created, while the Quran is the Word of Allah and it is not created.’ What do you say about avoiding him? And is he called an innovator? So he said: ‘This person is avoided and this is the saying of an innovator, and these are the words of the Jahmiyyah, and the Quran is not created.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, Chapter 3</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>Look at what the man in the question says. He affirms that the Quran is the Word of Allah and that it is uncreated. His only claim is that his own voice, breath and pronunciation are created, which is obvious since they come from him. For this he is to be avoided and is called an innovator.</p>

<blockquote>
  “186. I heard my father say: ‘Whoever says: My pronunciation of the Quran is created, then these are downright evil words and they are the words of the Jahmiyyah.’ I said to him: ‘Verily Al-Karabisi says this.’ So he said: ‘He has lied – <strong>may Allah rip him apart – dirty man</strong>.’ And he said to me: ‘He has verily taken the place of Bishr Al-Marisi.’ And my father used to hate saying anything about the pronunciation, or that it should be said ‘created’ or ‘not created’.”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 186</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>The footnote adds that Bishr Al-Marisi was one of the leaders of the Jahmiyyah. The report also contains a tension that is worth noting.</p>

<p>Ahmad “hated” saying anything at all about the pronunciation, either “created” or “not created,” and yet he is recorded as cursing a man who said it was created. The result is that believers are forbidden to say that their own recitation is created, and forbidden to say that it is uncreated, while the one man who said what is plainly true is cursed.</p>

<p>This was not a minor quarrel. The same dispute, known as the <em>lafz</em> controversy, cost the hadith master al-Bukhari his place in Nishapur.</p>

<p>He is reported to have said that his own articulation of the Quran is created, meaning the human act of reciting, while holding that the Quran itself is Allah’s uncreated speech. Prominent scholars of the city, among them al-Dhuhli, turned against him, and he was driven out.</p>

<p>He then wrote <em>Khalq Af‘al al-‘Ibad</em> (“The Creation of the Actions of the Servants”) to defend himself. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Bukhari">Wikipedia’s article on al-Bukhari</a> summarises the episode. So the compiler of the book that Sunnis rank highest after the Quran was himself treated as a suspect for saying what the man in Ahmad’s question said.</p>

<p>The sources also add that anyone who holds that anything belonging to Allah is created is beyond the pale:</p>

<blockquote>
  “179. Ibn Shubbawayh narrated to me: ‘Whoever says that anything from Allah is created: His Knowledge or His Words, then he is a zindiq and he is not prayed upon nor is he prayed behind, and his wealth is considered as the wealth of the murtad …’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 179</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>A <em>zindiq</em> who is not prayed over and whose wealth goes to the treasury is treated as an apostate. In other words, a person who believes in the Quran, prays, fasts and recites, but says that the sound of his own voice was made by Allah, loses the status of a Muslim.</p>

<h3>5. Calls to Behead Those Who Disagree</h3>

<p>What makes this so disturbing and troubling is that Muslims called for the murder, and in fact the beheading, of any person who would claim that the Quran, or their recitation of it, was created. These reports are not from fringe sources. They come from scholars whom the Sunni tradition honours and from a caliph.</p>

<blockquote>
  “193. … I heard Ibn Al-Mubarak say: ‘It’s a failure of the youth that there among them is no-one who can annihilate Bishr.’ …”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 193</cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote>
  “194. … I heard Shababah ibn Sawwir say: ‘My opinion and the opinion of Abu An-Nadr Hashim ibn Al-Qasim and a group of the fuqaha is that we agreed Al-Masiri is a kafir jahid. We believe he should be asked to repent. Then either he repents or else <strong>he is beheaded</strong>.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 194</cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote>
  “197. … I heard Harun, Amir Al-Muminin say: ‘It has reached to me that Bishr Al-Marisi claims that the Quran is created. I swear by Allah, if He lets me get my hands on him, <strong>I will kill him</strong> in a way that I have never killed anyone ever before.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 197</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>The “Harun” named here, with the title “Commander of the Faithful,” is evidently the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid. The statement is a vow to kill a man for his theological opinion, and it is made in the name of Allah. Note also that Harun al-Rashid died around 809, well before his son al-Ma’mun began the <em>mihna</em>. The father is here recorded threatening the same kind of violence that the son’s inquisition is later condemned for.</p>

<blockquote>
  “206. … I asked ‘Abdur-Rahman ibn Mahdi and said: ‘What do you say regarding the one who says: The Quran is created?’ So he said: ‘If I had authority to do it, then I would stand on a bridge, and then no man would pass by me except that I would ask him (regarding the Quran). The if he said: The Quran is created, <strong>I would behead him</strong> and throw his head in the water.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 206</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>Here a respected hadith scholar imagines himself as a roadblock on a bridge, questioning each passer-by about his creed and beheading those who give the wrong answer. A test of this kind does not look for a threat to the state. It looks for a thought.</p>

<blockquote>
  “211. … I heard ‘Abdul Malik ibn Al-Majishun say: ‘Whoever says that the Quran is created, he is a kafir.’ And I heard him (i.e. ‘Abdul-Malik) say: ‘If I found Bishr Al-Marisi then I would verily <strong>behead him</strong>.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 211</cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote>
  “214. … We were with Ibn ‘Uyaynah when the people became disordered around him. So Ibn ‘Uyaynah said: ‘What is this?’ So they said: ‘Bishr has arrived.’ So he said: ‘And what does he say?’ They said: ‘He says that the Quran is created.’ He said: ‘Bring him to me and bring me two witnesses so I can order the governor <strong>to behead him</strong>.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 214</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>In the same chapter the author also records that Harun al-Farwi said that whoever doubts the unbelief of the Waqifah is himself a kafir, which turns the charge of unbelief into a test that can be applied to anyone who is not severe enough. The Lafidhiyyah, who say only that their pronunciation is created, are “innovators and misguided.”</p>

<p>To be fair to the sources, not every report calls for execution. The report about Imam Malik, as narrated by ‘Abdullah ibn Nafi‘, states a milder penalty:</p>

<blockquote>
  “213. … ‘Abdullah (i.e. ibn Nafi) said that (Imam) Malik said: ‘Whoever says that the Quran is created, then he is disciplined and imprisoned until it is known that he made tawbah.’”
  <cite><em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em>, no. 213</cite>
</blockquote>

<p>But even this is a penalty for belief and not for any action. Imprisonment continues “until it is known” that the man has repented, which is a test that only a person’s heart can pass. The difference between Malik and the others is therefore one of degree: the others would end the man’s life, while Malik would remove his freedom.</p>

<p>A further point is often overlooked. The Mu‘tazilite side also used the state against its opponents: scholars were summoned, tested, imprisoned and flogged under the <em>mihna</em>, and Ahmad was one of those who suffered.</p>

<p>Sunni writers rightly condemn this. The reports above show, however, that the willingness to punish belief was not confined to the caliph’s court. The traditionists called for the same penalties when they imagined themselves holding power, and the only difference was which side had the sword.</p>

<h3>6. Why This Matters</h3>

<h4>1. A second uncreated thing next to Allah</h4>

<p>Islam presents itself as a strict monotheism, and Muslims often accuse Christians of making a creature into God. Yet the reports above say that the Quran stands on Allah’s side of the line between Creator and creation, that it is not Allah, that it is not other than Allah, and that anyone who places it among created things is an unbeliever. For a fuller account of how this doctrine is stated by Sunni institutions today, see {% if sib %}<a href="{{ sib.url | relative_url }}"><em>The Quran: Sunni Islam’s Multiple Gods?</em></a>{% else %}<em>The Quran: Sunni Islam’s Multiple Gods?</em>{% endif %}</p>

<p>Christians confess that the Word of God is a divine person who became flesh (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+1%3A1%2C+14&version=KJV">John 1:1, 14</a>), and Muslims object to this as a violation of God’s oneness. The early Sunni sources do something that looks very similar, since they declare that Allah’s Word is eternal, yet they do it for a book.</p>

<p>A Christian can at least point to a Person who is the Word. Here the thing that is eternal is a text that exists in print and sound.</p>

<p>Sunni theologians reply that an eternal <em>attribute</em> is not a second god, since an attribute has no existence apart from the one who has it. That reply is a serious one, and it is the reason the doctrine is stated in terms of Allah’s speech rather than of a book. The difficulty returns whenever the attribute is identified with the actual letters and sounds of the Quran, which is the position the early reports appear to defend.</p>

<h4>2. The creed is enforced by the sword and not by evidence</h4>

<p>Notice what is missing from the reports. None of the reports above quotes a verse or a saying of the Prophet that states, “The Quran is not created.” Even modern Sunni scholars concede that this debate did not arise in the age of the Companions (see the fatwa material in the earlier article linked above).</p>

<p>The doctrine is built by inference, and when inference met resistance, the answer was a threat of execution. That is a poor foundation for a belief that is meant to be known with certainty.</p>

<h4>3. The reports contradict later Sunni explanations</h4>

<p>The modern Sunni explanation is that there are two kinds of speech. The inner, eternal speech (<em>kalam nafsi</em>) is uncreated, and the letters, voice and ink (<em>kalam lafzi</em>) are created, though they point to the uncreated speech. This is exactly what the man in Ahmad’s question was saying when he affirmed that the Quran is the uncreated Word and that his own recitation is created, and Ahmad called him an innovator and said that his words are those of the Jahmiyyah.</p>

<p>The later view, then, was condemned by the very scholar whom Sunnis follow. And Muslims today cannot appeal to “Imam Ahmad” and also say that the recitation is created without contradicting him. Some later Hanbali scholars went the other way and held that the letters and sound of the Quran are themselves Allah’s eternal speech, which is the position the title of this article describes.</p>

<p>The point for readers of <em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em> is that the Sunni tradition has never had a single answer. The compilers of this book, who are Athari in outlook, treat the <em>kalam nafsi</em> solution as a deviation.</p>

<p>The Ash‘ari and Maturidi schools, who today represent a very large share of Sunni Muslims, treat the Athari wording as a risk of identifying Allah’s speech with created letters. Each side can quote Ahmad against the other.</p>

<h3>7. How Sunni Muslims Respond</h3>

<p>It is only right to set out the answers that Muslims give.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The mihna was a crisis.</strong> Muslims say that the strong language was a reaction to a state-backed campaign that imprisoned and flogged scholars for refusing to say that the Quran is created, so the harshness reflects the heat of persecution.</li>
  <li><strong>The statements are individual opinions.</strong> A wish to behead Bishr, they say, is the opinion of one man and not a ruling of the Muslim community or a verdict of a court.</li>
  <li><strong>Uncreated speech is an attribute.</strong> Sunnis argue that the Quran is Allah’s speech, and speech is an attribute of the speaker. An attribute of Allah cannot be created, so this does not amount to a second God.</li>
  <li><strong>The later distinction resolves the problem.</strong> The scholars who came afterwards distinguished the speech itself from its recitation, so that the printed Quran and the reciter’s voice are created while the speech is not.</li>
  <li><strong>Ahmad’s warning was about ambiguity.</strong> Some defenders say that Ahmad rejected both “my pronunciation is created” and “my pronunciation is not created” because the word <em>lafz</em> can mean either the recited Quran or the reciter’s own voice. Seen this way, he was refusing a loose phrase and not denying that human sounds are created.</li>
</ul>

<p>These are serious answers, but each leaves a question open. If the language was only a product of the crisis, why is it preserved in a book of creed as the model of correct belief?</p>

<p>The timeline above sharpens this question, since by the usual dates Malik, Ibn al-Mubarak, Harun al-Rashid, Ibn Mahdi and Ibn ‘Uyaynah had all died <em>before</em> the <em>mihna</em> began in 833. Their calls for punishment cannot have been a reaction to it.</p>

<p>If these were private opinions, why do the compilers present them as the “saying of the scholars,” with no dissent recorded? And if the later distinction is correct, then Ahmad, whom the later tradition honours, condemned a position that the tradition now holds.</p>

<p>The answer about ambiguity deserves a fair hearing, but it also has a cost. If the phrase was only unclear, the remedy was to explain it. Instead the sources record a man being avoided, a scholar being cursed, and another being driven out of his city for using it.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The reports in <em>Kitab As-Sunnah</em> show that the doctrine of the uncreated Quran was not a quiet theological preference. It was a boundary of belief: whoever said that the Quran is created was an unbeliever, whoever said that his own recitation is created was an innovator, whoever refused to answer was suspect, and the penalty for the first group was, in the words of several of the narrators, beheading.</p>

<p>The Quran is called Allah’s Word, and the early Sunni scholars drew the conclusion that it must be uncreated. But they refused to say that it is Allah, they refused to say that it is other than Allah, and they refused to let anyone say that even the sound of a human voice reciting it is a creature.</p>

<p>Those who find this position coherent should be asked to explain it. And those who find it troubling should note that the people who first set out the doctrine were not troubled by it enough to refrain from calling for the heads of their opponents.</p>

<h2>Glossary</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Ahl al-Hadith / Atharis:</strong> the traditionist camp around Ahmad ibn Hanbal, which rejects speculative theology and holds that Allah speaks in letters and sounds.</li>
  <li><strong>Ash‘aris and Maturidis:</strong> the two main theological schools of Sunni Islam in later centuries. Both hold that Allah’s speech is an inner, eternal meaning.</li>
  <li><strong>Jahmiyyah:</strong> followers of Jahm ibn Safwan; later a general label for anyone who said the Quran is created.</li>
  <li><strong>Kafir:</strong> an unbeliever. <strong>Murtad:</strong> an apostate. <strong>Zindiq:</strong> a heretic who is treated as an apostate.</li>
  <li><strong>Kalam nafsi / kalam lafzi:</strong> inner speech (the eternal meaning) and verbal speech (letters and sounds), the distinction used by the Ash‘aris and Maturidis.</li>
  <li><strong>Lafz:</strong> pronunciation or articulation, the word at the centre of the <em>lafz</em> controversy.</li>
  <li><strong>Mihna:</strong> “test” or “ordeal,” the inquisition begun by al-Ma’mun in 833.</li>
  <li><strong>Mu‘tazilites:</strong> a rationalist school that held the Quran to be created and had the support of al-Ma’mun.</li>
  <li><strong>Waqifah:</strong> “those who stop,” who would not say whether the Quran is created.</li>
</ul>
