<h2>Introduction</h2>

<p>The following article continues to draw on Gleason L. Archer Jr., <em>A Survey of Old Testament Introduction</em> (Chicago: Moody Press), pp. 195-203. It shows that <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1&version=ESV">Genesis 1</a> stands apart from the pagan creation myths in teaching creation out of nothing, that the case for an ancient earth rests on an assumption that has been challenged, and that the Hebrew word <em>yom</em>, “day,” allows more than one reading among sincere believers. This should trouble anyone who thinks the Bible must be set against science, or who thinks there is only one faithful way to read the days of creation.</p>

<p>With that said, I proceed to the summary.</p>

<h2>Main Discussion</h2>

<p>Each part below builds on the one before it, so it is best read in order.</p>

<h3>1. The Question</h3>

<p>A quick reading of Genesis 1 suggests that creation took place in six twenty-four-hour days, yet modern science says the earth is billions of years old. How should a believer in the Bible answer?</p>

<p>The source approaches this with three questions. How does Genesis compare with the pagan creation myths? What does the evidence for an old earth really rest on? And what can the Hebrew word <em>yom</em> mean?</p>

<h3>2. Genesis and the Pagan Myths</h3>

<p>For a long time scholars assumed that <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1&version=ESV">Genesis 1-2</a> was borrowed from pagan sources, especially the Babylonian creation epic <em>Enuma Elish</em>. More recent specialists such as Heidel, Lambert and Millard have rejected any close relationship between the two accounts.</p>

<p>The contrast is striking. <em>Enuma Elish</em> begins with matter that already exists and is eternal. Out of it, by forces that are never explained, a pair of creator gods arises and begins the work of creation. That does not answer how creation happened; it only pushes the question back a step. Where did the matter and the gods come from?</p>

<p>Genesis begins differently. There is one God, and everything else is His creation.</p>

<h4>Why <em>ex nihilo</em> matters</h4>

<p>There are good reasons to doubt that matter is eternal. Matter can be destroyed, as nuclear fission shows, and it has an intricate atomic structure that points to intelligent design. Only the Bible teaches that God created everything <em>ex nihilo</em>, out of nothing.</p>

<p>The alternative is hard to hold consistently. The idea that the universe came from nothing, without a cause, puts the atheist in a position of total irrationality.</p>

<p>As R. C. Sproul observes, the modern view is more miraculous than the biblical one, since it holds that nothing created everything. Every worldview must begin somewhere; the question is whether it begins with a Creator or with an unexplained accident.</p>

<h3>3. What Does Science Say About the Age of the Earth?</h3>

<p>Three kinds of evidence are usually given for an ancient earth.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Sedimentation.</strong> In the Gulf of Mexico layers are deposited at a few inches per year, yet layers as thick as 28,000 feet have been found, implying well over 100,000 years.</li>
  <li><strong>Fossils.</strong> Many fossils are of species no longer living and lie in strata that could not have formed in a single catastrophe like Noah’s flood.</li>
  <li><strong>Radioactive decay.</strong> Uranium 238 decays to lead 206 over about four and a half billion years, and rubidium 87 to strontium 87 over about sixty billion. Carbon 14 works only for much younger material.</li>
</ul>

<h4>The assumption behind the numbers</h4>

<p>All of these estimates depend on the <strong>uniformitarian</strong> assumption, that natural processes have always run at the same rate as today. A measurement of how fast something happens now tells us how long it took in the past only if the rate never changed.</p>

<p>That assumption has been seriously challenged. Harold Clark pointed to violent twisting and uplift in mountain chains such as the Alps and the Rockies, and to water action on a scale seen nowhere today. Even radiocarbon dating has needed correction, since tree rings from bristlecone pines show that past decay rates differed from the present rate.</p>

<p>The question therefore remains open. Can a huge span of time be reconciled with the six days of <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1&version=ESV">Genesis 1</a>? That depends on what <em>yom</em> means.</p>

<h3>4. Three Views of the Days</h3>

<p>Believers who take Genesis seriously have proposed three main answers.</p>

<h4>1. The Restoration (Gap) View</h4>

<p>The days are literal, but they describe the <em>restoration</em> of an earth already created in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A1&version=ESV">Genesis 1:1</a> and later thrown into chaos, possibly by the fall of Satan. The verb “was” in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A2&version=ESV">1:2</a> is read as “became.” Support is sought in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+45%3A18&version=ESV">Isaiah 45:18</a>, though the word <em>bohu</em> in the next verse plainly means “in vain.”</p>

<p>The view is exegetically possible, but it has two major difficulties. It shrinks the original creation to one verse, and it makes the Bible silent on the order of creation, so that there is nothing to reconcile between Genesis and geology. It has also attracted speculation, such as linking <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+28&version=ESV">Ezekiel 28</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah+4%3A23&version=ESV">Jeremiah 4:23</a> to a pre-catastrophe world with Satan as its ruler.</p>

<h4>2. The Revelational Day View</h4>

<p>Here the six days are the days in which God <em>revealed</em> the story of creation to Moses, perhaps in a vision. The stages need not be strictly chronological, but are arranged logically for the human observer. This does not necessarily surrender inerrancy.</p>

<p>But Genesis 1 reads as plain history and contains no hint of a vision. It seems unlikely that three verses about the creation of light would need a whole day to convey. And it is unclear how <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A1&version=ESV">Genesis 1:1</a>, which is not part of the first day, was communicated.</p>

<h4>3. The Day-Age View</h4>

<p>According to this view <em>yom</em> means a stage or age in the creative process. It was held by nineteenth-century geologists who respected the Bible, such as J. W. Dawson and James Dana. Three arguments are offered.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Genesis 2:4 uses “day” for the whole creation week.</strong> “In the day that the LORD God made earth and heaven” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+2%3A4&version=ESV">Genesis 2:4</a>) refers to the entire process of chapter 1.</li>
  <li><strong>The sixth day is overloaded.</strong> <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A27&version=ESV">Genesis 1:27</a> places the creation of Adam and Eve at the end of the sixth day, yet <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+2&version=ESV">Genesis 2</a> describes Adam tending the garden, naming the animals, feeling lonely, and then receiving Eve. All of that is hard to fit into a few hours.</li>
  <li><strong>“Evening and morning” does not prove a literal day.</strong> It can simply mark a twenty-four-hour cycle, just as “three days and three nights” is used of Christ’s burial across parts of three days.</li>
</ul>

<p>Its defenders note that Genesis and geology agree on several points: the earth began chaotic, conditions for life were prepared, land was separated from sea, plants came before animals, simpler forms came before complex ones, and man came last.</p>

<p>They add that the sun, moon and stars of the fourth day were not <em>made</em> for the first time: verse 16 uses the general verb <em>‘asah</em> and not <em>bara’</em>, so the point is that they became visible and useful for marking time, perhaps as a dense vapor thinned.</p>

<h3>5. An Objection and a Reply</h3>

<p>Some argue from <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus+20%3A11&version=ESV">Exodus 20:11</a>, “in six days the LORD made heaven and earth,” that the days were literal. But the point of the Sabbath command is memorial observance, and a literal seventh day is the only practical way to keep it. Scripture itself shows that a day can stand for a longer period, as in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus+23%3A42-43&version=ESV">Leviticus 23:42-43</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+4%3A4-5&version=ESV">Ezekiel 4:4-5</a>.</p>

<h3>6. Why This Matters</h3>

<p>The debate over the days can become heated, but notice what all three views share: God is the Creator, creation was purposeful, and Genesis 1 is truthful. Where believers differ is on the meaning of one Hebrew word. That is a question for careful study and mutual patience, not a test of who is faithful.</p>

<h3>7. Key Takeaways</h3>

<ul>
  <li>Genesis differs sharply from <em>Enuma Elish</em>: it teaches one God creating everything out of nothing, not eternal matter and rival gods.</li>
  <li>Estimates of an ancient earth rest on the uniformitarian assumption, which has been questioned.</li>
  <li>The gap, revelational and day-age views each try to relate Genesis 1 to the length of time, and each has strengths and difficulties.</li>
  <li>The word <em>yom</em> can mean a period of time, so sincere believers differ on the days.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>Genesis stands apart from the pagan myths in teaching creation out of nothing, and the length of the “days” remains a matter on which sincere believers differ. The deeper issue is whether God created at all. In <a href="{{ gp3.url | relative_url }}">PT 3</a> we weigh Darwinian evolution against the biblical record.</p>
