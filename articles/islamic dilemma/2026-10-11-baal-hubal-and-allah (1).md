## Introduction

What if the religious history of pre-Islamic Arabia reveals a connection between the ancient worship of Baal, the Meccan idol Hubal, and the name Allah?

The question deserves careful examination. Long before the rise of Islam, the people of Mecca participated in religious practices involving idols, divination, sacred objects, and multiple deities. Among the idols associated with the Kaaba was Hubal, whom several historical accounts describe as an important deity of the pre-Islamic Meccan sanctuary.

Meanwhile, the Hebrew Bible repeatedly condemns the worship of Baal, and the New Testament associates Beelzebul with Satan in the controversy surrounding the ministry of Jesus Christ.

These accounts raise a series of questions central to this investigation:

- What was Baal Zebub, and how does the New Testament connect Beelzebul with Satan?
- Was Hubal the chief deity of the Kaaba before Islam?
- What do Islamic historical traditions reveal about the religious practices of Muhammad's ancestors?
- Was Hubal connected with the ancient Semitic deity Baal?
- What is the evidence behind the argument that the Allah worshipped in pre-Islamic Mecca was associated with Hubal?

The argument developed in this article examines these questions through biblical passages, Islamic historical traditions, and the writings of authors who have discussed the origins of Arabian religion.

The central thesis is that the reported connections between these figures raise serious questions for Christian apologetic investigation. Whether those connections establish their complete identity, however, must be evaluated through the evidence presented.

## Baal Zebub of Ekron

The Hebrew Bible records an incident in which King Ahaziah of Israel sought an oracle from Baal Zebub, the god of Ekron.

Second Kings 1:1–3 recounts that, following an injury, Ahaziah sent messengers to inquire whether he would recover. The prophet Elijah confronted the messengers because the king had sought an answer from a foreign deity instead of the God of Israel.

The Lord's message through Elijah was clear: Ahaziah would die because of his decision to seek an oracle from Baal Zebub.

The NET Bible translates the relevant passage as follows:

> “You must think there is no God in Israel! That explains why you are on your way to seek an oracle from Baal Zebub the god of Ekron.” — 2 Kings 1:3 (NET)

Read the complete account in [2 Kings 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%201&version=NET).

The NET Bible provides an important explanatory note concerning the deity's name:

> “Apparently Baal Zebub refers to a local manifestation of the god Baal at the Philistine city of Ekron.” — NET Bible, note on 2 Kings 1:2

The note further explains that the name is commonly understood as “Lord of the Flies,” although it may reflect a deliberate scribal alteration of Baal Zebul, interpreted as “Baal, the Prince.”

Source: NET Bible, note on 2 Kings 1:2, [Bible Gateway](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%201&version=NET).

This passage establishes the biblical connection between Baal Zebub and the wider religious tradition associated with Baal.

But the investigation does not end in the Old Testament. The New Testament introduces another name that becomes particularly important in Christian discussions of Satan.

## Beelzebul and Satan

In the Gospels, Jesus Christ is accused by His opponents of casting out demons through Beelzebul, the ruler of demons.

Matthew records the accusation:

> “He does not cast out demons except by the power of Beelzebul, the ruler of demons!” — Matthew 12:24 (NET)

Jesus responds by exposing the contradiction in their reasoning:

> “So if Satan casts out Satan, he is divided against himself. How then will his kingdom stand?” — Matthew 12:26 (NET)

He continues by explaining that His works demonstrate the arrival of God's kingdom.

Mark 3:22–30 records a parallel account. The religious authorities accuse Jesus of being possessed by Beelzebul and casting out demons through the ruler of demons. Jesus responds by explaining that a kingdom divided against itself cannot stand.

Read the passages:

- [Matthew 12:24–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2012%3A24-32&version=NET)
- [Mark 3:22–30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%203%3A22-30&version=NET)
- [Matthew 10:24–25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010%3A24-25&version=NET)

Within this Gospel controversy, Beelzebul is closely associated with Satan, the ruler of demons.

The connection becomes especially significant when considered alongside the Old Testament's condemnation of Baal worship.

However, an important distinction must be maintained: these passages associate Beelzebul with Satan, but they do not explicitly state that every deity called Baal in ancient history is identical to Satan. The broader identification requires a theological argument beyond the wording of these passages alone.

For Christian apologetics, this is the biblical foundation upon which the discussion of Baal and Satan is developed.

## Hubal and the Kaaba

The next question concerns Hubal and his place in the religious life of pre-Islamic Mecca.

Several historical accounts describe Hubal as an important idol associated with the Kaaba before the rise of Islam.

In *Gods, Goddesses and Mythology*, Hubal is described as:

> “Chief god of the Ka‘ba; a martial and oracular deity; a moon god.” — C. Scott Littleton, ed., *Gods, Goddesses and Mythology* (Marshall Cavendish, 2005), vol. 11, p. 137

Malise Ruthven also discusses Hubal in *Islam in the World*:

> “The presiding deity was Hubal, a large carnelian kept inside the temple; 360 other idols were arranged outside…” — Malise Ruthven, *Islam in the World*, 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, 2000), p. 15

Ruthven further discusses the historical development of the Meccan sanctuary and its relationship to earlier Arabian religious traditions.

Similarly, Fred McGraw Donner describes the Kaaba within the religious environment of pre-Islamic Arabia.

Source: Fred McGraw Donner, *Muhammad and the Believers: At the Origins of Islam* (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2010), chapter 1, p. 35.

These accounts are relevant because they situate Hubal within the religious environment that preceded Islam.

The important question is not simply whether Hubal existed as an idol. It is whether his position in the Meccan sanctuary can help explain the religious terminology and practices reported in early Islamic historical traditions.

That question brings us to the account of Muhammad's grandfather.

## Abd al-Muttalib and Hubal

One of the most frequently discussed accounts concerning pre-Islamic religious practice appears in Ibn Ishaq's biography of Muhammad.

The narrative concerns Abd al-Muttalib, Muhammad's grandfather, and a vow involving the sacrifice of one of his sons.

According to the account, Abd al-Muttalib vowed that if God granted him ten sons who grew up and protected him, he would sacrifice one of them.

When his sons had grown up, Abd al-Muttalib assembled them and explained his vow. Each son supplied an arrow bearing his name so that the family could determine which son would be selected.

Ibn Ishaq's account then states:

> “They did so and went with them inside the ka‘ba to the site of their god Hubal…” — Ibn Ishaq, *The Life of the Prophet Muhammad*, pp. 125–127

The narrative explains that divining arrows were used to determine the outcome of important questions.

The account subsequently describes the family seeking the advice of a diviner and performing the prescribed procedure involving Abd Allah and camels.

At one point, Abd al-Muttalib is described as standing near Hubal while praying to God.

Source: Ibn Ishaq, *The Life of the Prophet Muhammad (Al-Sira al-Nabawiyya)*, translated by Trevor Le Gassick and reviewed by Ahmed Fareed, 1998, pp. 125–127.

### Why Is This Account Important?

The passage places several elements within the same narrative:

1. Abd al-Muttalib's appeal to God.
2. The Kaaba as the setting for the religious proceedings.
3. Hubal's idol and the divining arrows associated with it.
4. A ritual intended to determine the outcome of a vow.

This combination has led some Christian apologists to argue that Allah and Hubal occupied closely connected positions in pre-Islamic Meccan religion.

The argument raises a legitimate historical question: how did the pre-Islamic Arabs understand the relationship between their supreme deity, the idols they worshipped, and the rituals performed at the Kaaba?

Nevertheless, the narrative does not explicitly declare that Abd al-Muttalib believed Allah and Hubal were the same being. Standing near an idol while praying to God does not, on its own, establish that the two were identical.

The passage is therefore important evidence concerning the religious practices described in the traditional account, but the precise theological relationship between Allah and Hubal remains a separate question.

For the purposes of this article, the account is significant because it provides the historical context in which the proposed identification must be examined.

## Hubal and Baal

The proposed relationship between Hubal and Baal is another major component of this investigation.

John Muehleisen Arnold discusses the possibility that Hubal's name was connected with the Babylonian and Syrian Baal or Bel.

Arnold writes:

> “The opinion that it is synonymous with the Babylonian and Syrian Baal or Bel is supported by the testimony of Arab authorities, according to whom Hubal was originally imported from Syria.” — John Muehleisen Arnold, *Islam: Its History, Character, and Relation to Christianity*, chapter I, “The Land of Its Birth, The Pre-Islamite Kaaba,” pp. 26–27

This quotation presents an argument found in older literature on Islam and comparative religion.

Arnold's discussion is important to the history of the argument because it identifies a proposed connection between Hubal and the wider Semitic religious tradition associated with Baal or Bel.

However, the existence of this proposal does not mean that the identity of Hubal and Baal has been conclusively established. Arnold's interpretation must be evaluated alongside the underlying historical evidence and modern scholarship.

A related discussion appears in *Fabled Cities, Princes & Jinn from Arab Myths and Legends*.

The book states:

> “Hubal was associated with the Semitic god Ba‘l and with Adonis or Tammuz, the gods of spring, fertility, agriculture and plenty…” — Khairet al-Saleh, *Fabled Cities, Princes & Jinn from Arab Myths and Legends*, illustrations by Rashad N. Salim (Schocken Books, 1985), p. 28

These references establish that some authors have proposed or described a connection between Hubal and Baal.

For the Christian apologist, the question is whether this proposed connection, considered alongside the biblical treatment of Baal worship, has theological significance.

The argument becomes especially controversial when the proposed relationship is extended from Hubal to Allah and then from Baal to Satan.

## Hubal and Allah

The central argument presented in this article concerns the relationship between Hubal and Allah.

The historical literature identifies Hubal as an important deity associated with the Kaaba. The account of Abd al-Muttalib also places references to God and rituals involving Hubal within the same narrative.

Some Christian apologists interpret these details as evidence that the pre-Islamic Meccans associated Allah with Hubal.

Their reasoning is that, if Hubal occupied a central position in the sanctuary and Allah was invoked in connection with religious proceedings conducted there, the relationship between the two deserves serious investigation.

The argument becomes more forceful if independent evidence could establish that Hubal was regarded as another name or manifestation of Allah.

However, the historical passages cited here do not explicitly establish that identification.

The distinction matters because the word Allah is the Arabic term for God and is also used by Arabic-speaking Christians. Its use does not automatically identify the speaker's God with any particular pre-Islamic idol.

Likewise, the presence of Hubal at the Kaaba does not establish that every person who worshipped or invoked Allah regarded Hubal as Allah.

The historical question must therefore be framed precisely:

> Did the pre-Islamic Meccans explicitly identify Hubal with Allah, or did they recognize Allah as a deity while also worshipping Hubal and other figures?

The answer cannot be determined solely from the fact that both appear in accounts of the same religious environment.

Nevertheless, the issue remains relevant to investigations into the religious world in which Islam emerged.

## Idolatry and Spiritual Deception

The theological significance of this discussion becomes clearer when examined alongside the biblical condemnation of idolatry.

The Bible repeatedly warns Israel against worshipping foreign gods.

In 1 Corinthians 10:20, Paul writes:

> “The sacrifices of pagans are offered to demons, not to God.” — 1 Corinthians 10:20

Read [1 Corinthians 10:14–22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2010%3A14-22&version=NIV).

Paul's argument concerns the spiritual danger of participating in idolatrous worship. His point is not merely that idols are made of physical materials, but that pagan religious practices are incompatible with Christian allegiance to God.

The Hebrew Bible similarly condemns the worship of Baal and other deities.

In 2 Kings 1, Ahaziah is condemned for seeking an oracle from Baal Zebub rather than consulting the God of Israel.

In Matthew 12 and Mark 3, Jesus confronts accusations involving Beelzebul and identifies Satan as the ruler whose kingdom is under discussion.

Taken together, these passages provide a biblical framework for a Christian critique of idolatry and spiritual deception.

However, applying this framework to Hubal requires a further historical step: one must establish the identity of the deity being discussed and the nature of the worship associated with him.

If Hubal were conclusively demonstrated to be the same deity as Baal Zebub, the connection would raise significant theological questions for Christians. But the sources cited in this article do not conclusively demonstrate that historical identity.

The biblical principle remains clear even when a particular historical identification remains disputed: Christians are called to reject idolatry and worship the God revealed in Scripture.

## The Full Argument

The argument can now be presented in its strongest form as a sequence of claims requiring historical and theological evaluation.

1. **Premise 1:** The New Testament closely associates Beelzebul with Satan in the controversy surrounding Jesus' exorcisms.
2. **Premise 2:** The Hebrew Bible identifies Baal Zebub as a deity worshipped at Ekron and condemns Ahaziah's decision to seek an oracle from him.
3. **Premise 3:** Historical accounts identify Hubal as an important idol associated with the pre-Islamic Kaaba.
4. **Premise 4:** Some historical writers have proposed a connection between Hubal and the ancient Semitic deity Baal or Bel.
5. **Premise 5:** Traditional accounts of Abd al-Muttalib place references to God and rituals involving Hubal within the same narrative.
6. **Premise 6:** The precise relationship between Allah and Hubal in pre-Islamic Meccan religion requires further historical investigation.

The more controversial conclusion is that Allah, Hubal, Baal, and Satan are all identical.

That conclusion depends on establishing several additional propositions: that Hubal was actually identical to Baal, that Hubal was identified with Allah, and that the biblical association between Beelzebul and Satan applies to the particular deity identified as Hubal.

The sources discussed above do not conclusively establish every link in that chain.

Nevertheless, the argument identifies an important area of inquiry for Christian apologetics: the relationship between the religious traditions of pre-Islamic Arabia, the history of the Kaaba, and the theological claims made by Islam.

A rigorous investigation should pursue these questions through primary historical sources, linguistic evidence, and biblical interpretation.

## The Apologetic Question

For Christian apologetics, the ultimate question is not simply whether the word Allah was used before Islam or whether Hubal was worshipped at the Kaaba.

The deeper question concerns the identity of God and the truth of the revelation presented in Christianity and Islam.

The New Testament declares:

> “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” — John 1:1 (NET)

It continues:

> “And the Word became flesh and took up residence among us.” — John 1:14 (NET)

Christians understand these passages as central to the doctrine of the incarnation of the Son of God.

Christian-Muslim disagreements about Jesus, His divine identity, His crucifixion, and His resurrection can be investigated directly through the relevant biblical and Qur'anic texts.

Consequently, the historical investigation of Hubal and pre-Islamic Arabian religion should complement, rather than replace, a direct examination of the theological differences between Christianity and Islam.

The strongest Christian apologetic combines careful historical investigation with a clear presentation of biblical teaching.

## Conclusion

The proposed connection between Baal, Hubal, Allah, and Satan remains a controversial subject in Christian-Muslim apologetics.

The biblical sources condemn the worship of Baal Zebub and associate Beelzebul with Satan. Historical accounts identify Hubal as an important idol in pre-Islamic Mecca, while some older writers propose a relationship between Hubal and Baal.

The traditional account of Abd al-Muttalib further raises questions about the religious practices associated with the Kaaba before Islam.

These elements make the subject worthy of serious investigation.

However, the conclusion that Allah, Hubal, Baal, and Satan are identical requires more evidence than the cited passages alone provide.

For Christian apologists, the proper response is neither to ignore the history of pre-Islamic Arabian religion nor to treat every proposed connection as established fact.

Instead, the evidence should be examined carefully, the historical claims should be distinguished from theological interpretations, and the competing claims about God should be evaluated in light of Scripture and reliable historical research.

The central question remains:

> What does the historical evidence reveal about the religious world of pre-Islamic Mecca, and how should Christians understand that history in relation to the biblical revelation of God?

That question deserves a serious answer grounded in Scripture, history, and sound reasoning.
