Cain, the Fallen Angels and the Flood

Anthony William · 15 September 2026

A study of Genesis 6: the three main interpretations of the sons of God, the biblical teaching on fallen angels, the flood narrative, and its use in the New Testament.

Introduction

Genesis 4 and 5 present two families: the line of Cain, which ends in Lamech's song of vengeance, and the line of Seth, which includes Enoch and ends with Noah. Genesis 6 begins by describing an encounter between the "sons of God" and the "daughters of man," and then states the cause of the flood. The passage is brief and syntactically compressed, and it has produced more interpretive literature than almost any other paragraph of the primeval history. This study sets out the three principal interpretations, examines the evidence for each, places the discussion within the wider biblical teaching on fallen angels, and then considers the flood narrative and its use in the New Testament.

The historical question and the theological question need to be kept distinct. The historical question is how ancient readers understood the passage, and here the evidence is relatively abundant. The theological question is what Christians should affirm, and here the passage permits more than one answer. The Bible never says that the Nephilim were descendants of Cain, and the interpretations below are presented with the strengths and weaknesses that scholars attribute to them.

Genesis 6:1-4 and its interpretation

The text reads:

"When man began to multiply on the face of the land and daughters were born to them, the sons of God saw that the daughters of man were attractive. And they took as their wives any they chose." — Genesis 6:1-2 (ESV)

Verse 4 adds that "the Nephilim were on the earth in those days, and also afterward, when the sons of God came in to the daughters of man, and they bore children to them. These were the mighty men who were of old, the men of renown." The term Nephilim is usually derived from nāpal, "to fall," but the etymology is uncertain, and the Septuagint translates the word as gigantes, "giants" (Wenham 1987; Hendel 1987). Three interpretations have been dominant in the history of exegesis.

The angelic interpretation

On the first reading, the "sons of God" (bĕnê hā'ĕlōhîm) are heavenly beings. The phrase has this meaning in Job 1:6, Job 2:1 and Job 38:7, and in related forms in Psalm 29:1 and Psalm 89:6, so the interpretation follows the ordinary usage of the expression in the Hebrew Bible. It is also the earliest attested reading. The Book of the Watchers (1 Enoch 1-36), whose Aramaic fragments from Qumran date to about 200 BC and whose composition is usually placed in the third century BC, tells how a group of angels descended, took human wives, and fathered giants (Nickelsburg 1977; 2001). The same tradition appears in Jubilees 5, in the Book of Giants and other texts from Qumran, in the Genesis Apocryphon, which reports Lamech's anxiety that Noah might have been fathered by a Watcher, and in Josephus (Antiquities 1.3.1). Among early Christian writers Justin Martyr (Second Apology 5) and Tertullian (On the Apparel of Women 1.2) followed it.

The New Testament appears to depend on this tradition. Jude 6-7 speaks of angels who "did not stay within their own position of authority, but left their proper dwelling," and are kept "in eternal chains under gloomy darkness until the judgment of the great day." It then sets beside them Sodom and Gomorrah, which "likewise indulged in sexual immorality and pursued unnatural desire" (verse 7), and Jude 14-15 quotes 1 Enoch directly. 2 Peter 2:4-5 places the sinning angels and the flood in a single sentence, and Richard Bauckham (1983) argues that both letters draw on Enochic tradition, a conclusion that does not require the whole of 1 Enoch to be treated as Scripture. Some interpreters add 1 Peter 3:19-20, although that passage is among the most disputed in the New Testament.

The chief objection is Matthew 22:30, where Jesus says that in the resurrection people "neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven." Defenders reply that Jesus describes angels "in heaven" and says nothing about angels who left their place. A second objection is theological: it is not obvious how spiritual beings could have fathered children, and the ancient sources themselves offer different explanations.

The Sethite interpretation

The second reading holds that the "sons of God" were the godly descendants of Seth who married the women of Cain's line. The earliest surviving Christian witness is usually said to be Julius Africanus in the third century, and the view became dominant through John Chrysostom (Homilies on Genesis 22) and Augustine (City of God 15.22-23), who rejected the angelic reading; Annette Reed (2005) traces the history of this shift. It also won the support of John Calvin. Its attractions are the contrast between the two lines in Genesis 4-5 and the Old Testament's use of "sons of God" for God's people (Deuteronomy 14:1; Hosea 1:10).

The difficulties are textual. Genesis 6:1-2 speaks of "man" and "the daughters of man" in general, and nothing in the context identifies the sons of God with Seth's descendants. It is also unclear why marriages between the two lines would produce the "mighty men" of verse 4. Reed argues that the Sethite reading arose in part as a polemical response to the growing authority of the Enochic tradition, which suggests that its popularity reflects the theological concerns of late antiquity as well as exegesis.

The royal interpretation

The third reading takes the sons of God to be rulers who abused their power by taking wives at will. Jewish interpreters adopted it early: the Aramaic Targum Onqelos renders the phrase as "sons of the great ones," Rashi explains it as "sons of nobles and judges," and Genesis Rabbah 26 attributes to Rabbi Simeon bar Yohai a curse on those who call the figures "sons of God." In modern scholarship Meredith Kline (1962) argued for the royal reading on the ground that kings are called God's sons in the Old Testament (see Psalm 82:6). Others treat the passage as a fragment of an older myth of demigods that the narrator reworked to introduce the flood (Hendel 1987; Westermann 1984).

An assessment

Wenham (1987) concludes that the angelic reading best fits the language of the text, and many other commentators prefer the Sethite or royal readings, so the question remains open. Three points should be made with confidence. First, nothing in the text connects the Nephilim with Cain; Genesis 6:4 says that they were on the earth "in those days, and also afterward," and the spies in Numbers 13:33 later report seeing Nephilim in Canaan. Second, even a reading that associates Genesis 6 with Cain's family would not make Cain an angel or a demon. Third, 1 Enoch is not part of the Jewish or Protestant canon, and although Jude 14-15 cites it, the citation does not give canonical status to the whole book, a point on which the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Churches differ. Whichever view is adopted, the narrator's purpose is clear: the passage describes the escalation of human corruption to the point where judgment becomes necessary.

Fallen angels elsewhere in the Bible

The angelic reading of Genesis 6 does not stand alone, since the Bible teaches elsewhere that some angels rebelled. Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 say that God did not spare angels who sinned, and the Greek verb in 2 Peter (tartarōsas) means to confine in Tartarus, a term from Greek cosmology that does not appear elsewhere in the New Testament (Bauckham 1983). The Book of Revelation describes "the great dragon... that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan" being thrown down to the earth (Revelation 12:9). These passages allow the conclusion that Cain's violence, the rebellion of angels and the corruption of Noah's generation are connected in the biblical writers' thinking, even though no text describes the connection in detail.

The flood

The narrator states the cause of the flood in terms that connect it with Cain's story:

"The LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intention of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually." — Genesis 6:5 (ESV)

The Hebrew word for "violence" (ḥāmās) in Genesis 6:11 and 6:13 recalls the shed blood of Genesis 4:10 and Lamech's boast. The flood is therefore presented as the judgment on a world in which the pattern begun by Cain has become universal. The narrative also stresses mercy. Noah "found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (Genesis 6:8), and the text describes him as "a righteous man, blameless in his generation" who "walked with God" (Genesis 6:9), the same expression used of Enoch. The New Testament calls him "a herald of righteousness" (2 Peter 2:5) and says that he built the ark "by faith" (Hebrews 11:7).

Comparison with the Mesopotamian flood traditions is instructive. In the Atrahasis epic the flood is sent because human noise disturbs the god Enlil, and in the Epic of Gilgamesh the gods' decision is not clearly justified and is criticised by the god Ea. Genesis shares the memory of a great flood but gives it a moral cause and a single divine author (Walton 2006). It also ends with a covenant, which the ancient Near Eastern accounts lack. That covenant includes a principle that answers Cain's crime: "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image" (Genesis 9:6). The sacredness of human life is grounded in the image of God, and the rainbow becomes the sign of the promise (Genesis 9:12-17).

The New Testament uses the flood in three ways: as a warning of coming judgment (Matthew 24:37-39; Luke 17:26-27), as evidence that God judges the ungodly (2 Peter 3:5-7), and as a type of salvation through water (1 Peter 3:20-21).

Cain, Abel and Christ

The Bible does not leave Cain's story without a theological response. Genesis 4:10 describes Abel's blood as crying out from the ground, and Hebrews 12:24 says that the blood of Jesus "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel." The comparison sets the cry for justice against the proclamation of forgiveness. It also connects the story with the promise of Genesis 3:15, which the New Testament sees fulfilled in Christ, "the reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8).

Conclusion

The identity of the sons of God remains disputed, but the argument of Genesis 6 does not depend on resolving it: the passage describes a world in which power is used for violence and self-indulgence, and the narrator treats that condition as the ground of the flood. The flood did not end the problem, however. Noah's descendants carried the human capacity for pride into the new world, and the table of nations, the figure of Nimrod and the story of Babel show how it reappeared. The next study turns to those chapters.
