## Jewish View on Daniel 7

Daniel 7 is one of the most important passages in discussions about whether the Messiah—and, in Christian interpretation, Jesus Christ—shares in divine identity. The vision presents two striking figures: the **Ancient of Days**, seated in judgment, and “one like a son of man,” who comes with the clouds of heaven and receives dominion, glory, and a kingdom. The central question is not simply whether the second figure is exalted. He plainly is. The harder question is what kind of figure he represents: the people of Israel, a heavenly angelic representative, the Messiah, or a figure who participates in God’s own heavenly rule.

A responsible reading should begin with the Jewish interpretive history rather than assume that Daniel 7 has only one possible meaning. Jewish interpreters have not spoken with one voice on the identity of the “one like a son of man.” The figure has been understood collectively, as a symbol of the faithful or holy ones; individually, as a messianic figure; and angelically, in some interpretations as Michael or Israel’s heavenly representative. The *Jewish Encyclopedia* surveys several of these proposals, while the USCCB’s commentary likewise notes that scholars differ over whether the figure represents God’s people, Michael, or the Messiah. [Jewish Encyclopedia, “Man, Son of”](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10342-man-son-of); [USCCB, Daniel 7](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/7).

This range of interpretations matters. It prevents us from treating the phrase “son of man” as if it automatically meant “God” in every context. At the same time, the existence of collective and angelic readings does not by itself settle the question against a messianic or divine interpretation. The argument must be made from the details of the vision, its Jewish interpretive background, and the way later Jewish and Christian readers understood it.

## The Vision: The Ancient of Days and One Like a Son of Man

Daniel sees thrones set in place and the Ancient of Days take his seat. His clothing is white, his hair is like pure wool, and his throne is associated with fire. The court sits, and the books are opened. Then Daniel sees “one like a son of man” coming with the clouds of heaven. This figure is brought before the Ancient of Days and is given dominion, glory, and a kingdom, so that peoples, nations, and languages serve him; his dominion is described as everlasting and his kingdom as one that will not pass away. Read the chapter directly in [Daniel 7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Daniel%207&version=KJV).

Two features deserve particular attention. First, the “son of man” figure is distinct from the Ancient of Days in the scene: he approaches and receives authority from him. Second, the authority he receives is extraordinarily comprehensive and enduring. The text therefore presents distinction between the figures, but also places the son-of-man figure in a position of exceptional heavenly authority.

That combination is the heart of the debate. Distinction does not necessarily mean that the recipient is merely human, just as exaltation does not automatically prove that the recipient is God. Daniel’s vision must be read carefully: it portrays a heavenly figure who receives authority from the Ancient of Days, but the passage does not use a simple formula saying that the two figures are one person or that they are equal in every respect.

## The Son of Man as Israel, Messiah, or Heavenly Angel

One major Jewish approach reads the “one like a son of man” collectively. On this reading, the humanlike figure symbolizes the “holy ones of the Most High,” who are vindicated after the oppressive kingdoms represented by beasts. The contrast is between beastlike empires and a humanlike kingdom belonging to God’s faithful people. The figure can therefore represent Israel—or the faithful community—without being a separate divine person.

A second approach understands the figure individually, as a Messiah or representative of the people. A symbolic figure may stand for a community while also being represented by an individual ruler. This possibility helps explain why later Jewish and Christian traditions could read the passage messianically without all agreeing on precisely how the figure relates to the people he represents.

A third approach identifies the figure with a heavenly angelic representative, often associated in scholarship with Michael. This is not a trivial alternative: in Jewish apocalyptic thought, heavenly beings can represent nations or communities before God. Philip Munoa’s study of Daniel 7:13–14 examines the passage in relation to Israel’s angel traditions and the possibility of an angelic interpretation. See [Munoa, “The Son of Man and the Angel of the Lord”](https://doi.org/10.1177/0951820718823392). The *Jewish Encyclopedia* also discusses the possibility that the figure is Israel’s guardian angel, Michael. [Jewish Encyclopedia, “Man, Son of”](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/10342-man-son-of).

These interpretations show why it is inaccurate to claim that Jewish tradition unanimously identified Daniel’s son of man as either the Messiah or God. The evidence is diverse. But the diversity is also historically important: Daniel 7 created room for arguments about a heavenly representative who could receive authority and honour in a way that ordinary human rulers do not.

## The “Two Powers in Heaven” Background

Alan F. Segal’s *Two Powers in Heaven* is important for understanding why Daniel 7 became a sensitive text in Jewish debates about divine plurality. In his analysis of rabbinic traditions, Segal observes that the rabbis preserved disputes in which interpreters appealed to biblical passages that appeared to suggest plurality in heaven. The rabbinic responses often placed a singular expression near a plural one, using the surrounding context to defend the unity of God. Segal cautions, however, that the surviving rabbinic reports are abbreviated and polemical; they do not necessarily reproduce the full sophistication of the positions being opposed.

In the traditions discussed by Segal, Daniel 7:9 is especially significant. The rabbinic list mentions the placement of “thrones” before the Ancient of Days. Segal argues that the original issue behind this example likely involved more than the grammar of a plural noun: it concerned the two heavenly manifestations in the vision, the Ancient of Days and the “son of man.” In other words, Daniel 7 could be read as presenting more than one exalted figure in the heavenly scene, and this raised questions for interpreters concerned to protect strict divine unity. See Alan F. Segal, *Two Powers in Heaven: Early Rabbinic Reports about Christianity and Gnosticism* (Brill, 1977), especially pp. 121–134; [book record at JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvp2n4kr).

This point must be expressed carefully. Segal’s discussion does not prove that every group that read Daniel 7 in a plural or two-figure way was Christian, nor does it prove that the rabbis were directly answering the later Christian doctrine of the Trinity in every instance. The traditions refer broadly to *minim*—a rabbinic label for opponents or sectarians whose precise identity varies by context. Segal’s larger point is that these traditions preserve traces of debates over a second heavenly figure and over how biblical language could be used to describe God’s activity or presence.

The rabbinic evidence is therefore valuable, but it has limits. It tells us that Daniel 7 could become a contested text in arguments about heavenly plurality. It does not, by itself, establish the exact beliefs of every group involved or decide the theological question for modern readers.

## Does Daniel 7 Identify the Son of Man as Divine?

The strongest argument for a high or divine status for the son-of-man figure begins with the imagery and the authority he receives. He comes with the clouds of heaven, is presented before the Ancient of Days, and receives an everlasting dominion over all peoples and nations. The universal and eternal character of the kingdom makes the figure much greater than an ordinary earthly king. In the wider biblical tradition, cloud imagery is often associated with divine presence, although imagery alone cannot settle the figure’s identity.

The next question concerns the language of service. Daniel 7:14 says that peoples and nations will serve the figure. The meaning of the underlying Aramaic term and its use in context are important to the discussion; readers should not base the whole argument on an English translation alone. What can be said securely is that the figure receives universal allegiance within the vision, and his dominion is everlasting. These features explain why later interpreters saw the passage as capable of supporting a very elevated view of the Messiah.

Nevertheless, there is a distinction between saying that Daniel 7 portrays an exalted heavenly ruler and saying that it explicitly teaches the later Christian doctrine that Christ is co-equal and co-eternal with the Father. Daniel 7 does not present that later doctrinal formula. Christian theologians argue that the passage contributes to a broader biblical case for Christ’s divine identity when read alongside other texts; Jewish interpreters who reject that conclusion commonly understand the figure as a symbol of Israel, a messianic agent, or an angelic representative. The passage is central to the debate, but it is not a substitute for the wider argument.

## Daniel 7 and Jesus’ Use of “Son of Man”

The New Testament makes Daniel 7 particularly important because Jesus is repeatedly described as the “Son of Man.” In the trial narrative in Matthew, Jesus speaks of the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven. That language strongly recalls Daniel’s vision and connects Jesus’ identity with the heavenly figure who receives dominion. Read [Matthew 26:57–68](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026%3A57-68&version=KJV) alongside [Daniel 7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Daniel%207&version=KJV).

For Christian interpretation, this is a major link: Jesus is not merely using “Son of Man” as a generic expression for humanity; in this setting, he invokes imagery of heavenly vindication, authority, and coming on the clouds. The high priest’s reaction in Matthew’s account shows that the statement was understood as a momentous claim, though interpreters disagree over whether the charge is best described as blasphemy, messianic self-identification, or an assertion of divine status.

For a Jewish reading that does not accept Jesus as Messiah, the New Testament’s application of Daniel 7 to Jesus is a later Christian interpretation, not proof that the original passage unambiguously identified Jesus by name. This distinction between the original literary setting and its later reception is essential to serious historical analysis. Christians may argue that Jesus fulfils the vision; Jewish interpreters may reject that claim while still recognizing the passage’s messianic or heavenly dimensions.

## What the Evidence Allows Us to Conclude

Daniel 7 does not offer a simple, uncontested answer to the question of Christ’s deity. It presents the Ancient of Days and a distinct humanlike heavenly figure who receives everlasting dominion and universal allegiance. Jewish interpretation has understood this figure in several ways: collectively as the faithful people, individually as a Messiah or representative, and angelically as a heavenly guardian such as Michael. The existence of these readings should be acknowledged rather than suppressed.

Segal’s analysis adds another layer: rabbinic traditions show that Daniel 7 was among the passages associated with disputes about more than one exalted figure in heaven. The rabbis’ responses reveal concern about readings that could be taken to imply divine plurality, but their polemical summaries do not provide a complete account of all the groups or doctrines they opposed. The evidence supports the conclusion that Daniel 7 was contested; it does not justify assigning every interpretation to one religious movement.

The Christian case for Christ’s deity therefore rests on a cumulative reading. Daniel 7 supplies a powerful picture of the Son of Man’s heavenly authority and everlasting kingdom; the New Testament applies this imagery to Jesus; and Christian theology reads this alongside other passages to formulate its account of Christ’s relation to God. A Jewish interpretation may accept the exalted figure while understanding him as symbolic, messianic but subordinate, or angelic. The disagreement is real and historically deep.

The most defensible conclusion is neither that Daniel 7 plainly and explicitly states the full later doctrine of the Trinity, nor that the chapter has nothing to contribute to Christian claims about Jesus. Its vision of a heavenly figure distinct from the Ancient of Days yet granted universal and everlasting dominion became fertile ground for Jewish and Christian debate about the identity, authority, and status of the Messiah. The text’s force lies precisely in this combination of distinction and extraordinary exaltation.
