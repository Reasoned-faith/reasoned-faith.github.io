<h2>Introduction</h2>

<p>The doctrine of the Trinity tells us who Jesus Christ is. He is the eternal Son of God, the Second Person of the Holy Trinity, and therefore fully God. That answer raises a further question. God is spirit, invisible and immortal. Christ lived among us in a visible body, grew tired, suffered and died. How can one and the same Jesus be both?</p>

<p>This article looks at that question step by step. It begins with what Scripture says about God’s invisibility, then explains what the Bible means when God “appears”, then looks at the early error that denied Christ’s real humanity, and finally sets out how the Church explains the Incarnation.</p>

<h2>Main Discussion</h2>

<p>Each part below builds on the one before it, so it is best read in order.</p>

<h3>1. The Puzzle: God Is Invisible, Yet Christ Was Seen</h3>

<p>The Trinity answers the question of <em>who</em> came to save us. The Incarnation answers the question of <em>how</em> He came. The two doctrines belong together, and neither is complete without the other.</p>

<p>The puzzle is real, because the Bible states both sides plainly. First, God is not like us:</p>

<blockquote>
  “God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%204%3A24&version=KJV">John 4:24 (KJV)</a></cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote>
  “Who only hath immortality, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto; whom no man hath seen, nor can see.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Timothy%206%3A16&version=KJV">1 Timothy 6:16 (KJV)</a></cite>
</blockquote>

<p>Second, the New Testament says that the Son entered human history. He was seen, touched and heard, and He died on a cross. Both statements are true. The Incarnation is the Church’s explanation of how they fit together.</p>

<h3>2. When the Bible Says God “Appeared”</h3>

<p>Scripture often describes God speaking to people and even appearing to them. God appeared to Abraham as three men (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2018&version=KJV">Genesis 18</a>), which many Christians understand as a hint of the Trinity. The Holy Spirit came down like a dove at Jesus’ baptism (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%203%3A22&version=KJV">Luke 3:22</a>) and appeared as tongues of fire at Pentecost (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%202%3A3-4&version=KJV">Acts 2:3-4</a>).</p>

<p>These passages do not mean that God has a voice box, wings or flames. The same Scripture says that God has no physical body (compare <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%204%3A24&version=KJV">John 4:24</a> with <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2024%3A39&version=KJV">Luke 24:39</a>) and that He is invisible (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians%201%3A15&version=KJV">Colossians 1:15</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Timothy%201%3A17&version=KJV">1 Timothy 1:17</a>).</p>

<p>In these episodes God’s presence was shown through signs, forms and voices that served for a time and then ceased. They represented God without being His own nature.</p>

<p>This is why the question about Christ is so pressing. If every earlier “appearance” was a temporary sign, was Jesus simply another one?</p>

<h3>3. The Early Error: Docetism</h3>

<p>Some early teachers answered yes. They taught that Christ only <em>seemed</em> to be human, that His body was a visible shape through which God showed Himself, and that His suffering and death were an illusion. This view is called <strong>Docetism</strong>, from a Greek word meaning “to seem.”</p>

<p>The apostles rejected it firmly. John makes the confession of Christ’s real humanity a test of true teaching:</p>

<blockquote>
  “Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20John%204%3A2&version=KJV">1 John 4:2 (KJV)</a></cite>
</blockquote>

<p>The risen Jesus also invited His disciples to examine Him: “Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2024%3A39&version=KJV">Luke 24:39</a>). The Church has always taught the opposite of Docetism. Christ was truly human, made of flesh and blood. He truly suffered, truly died and was truly raised from the dead.</p>

<h3>4. Christ’s Real Humanity in Scripture</h3>

<p>The Bible describes Jesus as having everything that belongs to a complete human being:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>A real body.</strong> “The Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%201%3A14&version=KJV">John 1:14</a>). He shared in flesh and blood (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%202%3A14&version=KJV">Hebrews 2:14</a>).</li>
  <li><strong>A human soul and spirit.</strong> He said, “My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026%3A38&version=KJV">Matthew 26:38</a>).</li>
  <li><strong>Human emotions.</strong> At the tomb of Lazarus He “groaned in the spirit, and was troubled” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2011%3A33&version=KJV">John 11:33</a>).</li>
  <li><strong>Real temptation.</strong> He was “in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%204%3A15&version=KJV">Hebrews 4:15</a>).</li>
  <li><strong>Likeness to His brothers.</strong> He was made “like unto his brethren” in all things (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%202%3A17&version=KJV">Hebrews 2:17</a>).</li>
</ul>

<p>Nothing here is a disguise. The Son became a real man.</p>

<h3>5. God Does Not Change, So How Did the Son Become Man?</h3>

<p>The second difficulty is that God does not change. “I am the LORD, I change not” (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi%203%3A6&version=KJV">Malachi 3:6</a>; see also <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%201%3A10-12&version=KJV">Hebrews 1:10-12</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=James%201%3A17&version=KJV">James 1:17</a>). So when the Son became man, He could not have stopped being God.</p>

<p>For this reason the Church teaches that the Incarnation was not a <em>transformation</em>. The Second Person did not turn into a human being, as if God had become something else. Instead, the Son <em>took on</em> a human nature in addition to His divine nature. Paul describes this as self-giving, not self-loss:</p>

<blockquote>
  “Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: But made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men.”
  <cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians%202%3A6-7&version=KJV">Philippians 2:6-7 (KJV)</a></cite>
</blockquote>

<p>He did not give up being God. He added what He was not: a complete human nature. After the Incarnation the Son has two natures, the divine nature He has always had and the human nature He received from the Virgin Mary.</p>

<h3>6. How the Incarnation Happened</h3>

<p>According to the faith of the Church, God created a human being in the womb of the Virgin Mary, and this man, Jesus, belonged from the first moment of his existence to the Second Divine Person. There was never a human Jesus who existed on his own and was later joined to the Son. From conception onward, this man <em>is</em> the Son of God living a human life.</p>

<p>All three persons of the Trinity took part in this act, as they do in every act of creation. But only one of them became incarnate:</p>

<ul>
  <li>The <strong>Son</strong> took on a human nature and now has two natures, divine and human.</li>
  <li>The <strong>Father</strong> and the <strong>Holy Spirit</strong> were not incarnated. They remain in the divine nature alone.</li>
</ul>

<p>This is why Christians say that the Son became man, not that God as a whole became man.</p>

<h3>7. Why the Incarnation Matters</h3>

<p>This doctrine is not a puzzle for philosophers only. It is the foundation of salvation.</p>

<p>Humanity needed a Savior who could truly represent human beings and truly die for them, and who could also defeat sin and death by divine power. The letter to the Hebrews joins the two ideas: He had to be made like His brothers in every respect, so that He could be a merciful and faithful high priest and make atonement for the sins of the people (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%202%3A17&version=KJV">Hebrews 2:17</a>).</p>

<p>The early Fathers summed up this logic in a short saying: what the Son did not take on, He could not heal. If He had only seemed to be human, our humanity would not have been redeemed. If He had been only a man, His sacrifice would not have had infinite worth.</p>

<h3>8. Four Common Misunderstandings</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>“God turned into a man.”</strong> No. God does not change. The Son added a human nature without ceasing to be God.</li>
  <li><strong>“Christ was half God and half man.”</strong> No. He is not a mixture or a blend. He is fully God and fully man.</li>
  <li><strong>“Jesus only looked human.”</strong> No. That is the Docetist error. His body, soul and suffering were real.</li>
  <li><strong>“The Father became man and died.”</strong> No. Only the Son was incarnated. The Father and the Spirit were not.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

<p>The Trinity teaches that Jesus is the eternal Son of God. The Incarnation teaches that this Son truly became one of us, without ceasing to be what He was. Together they give the full Christian answer to the question, “Who was Jesus Christ?” He is the invisible God made visible, the eternal one living a mortal life, and the Creator entering His own creation to save it.</p>
