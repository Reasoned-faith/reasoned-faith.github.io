Fallen Angels, False Gods and the Nations

Anthony William · 10 October 2026

A study of the nations and their gods: the textual and interpretive evidence from Scripture, the early Jewish and Christian explanations, the history of Zeus and Odin, and the biblical response in Christ.

Introduction

According to Genesis, the dispersion at Babel divided humanity into nations and languages, and the Bible and the surrounding ancient world agree that the nations soon had gods of their own: Zeus among the Greeks, Jupiter among the Romans, Woden or Odin among the Germanic and Norse peoples. How these gods arose, and what the worship offered to them involved, has been answered in three main ways. According to the first, spiritual beings, whether fallen angels or demons, stood behind the worship of the nations. According to the second, the gods were human constructions, personified forces of nature or deified ancestors and kings. According to the third, both explanations are partly true. This final study sets out what the Bible says, examines the writings of the early Jewish and Christian authors, and considers the historical evidence for two well-documented cases, Zeus and Odin. Throughout, the study distinguishes between what the texts state and what scholars infer, and it notes where the evidence permits more than one conclusion.

The nations and their gods in the Old Testament

The central text is a verse in the Song of Moses whose reading is disputed:

"When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance, when he divided mankind, he fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God. But the LORD's portion is his people, Jacob his allotted heritage." — Deuteronomy 32:8-9 (ESV)

The Masoretic Text, the traditional Hebrew text, reads "sons of Israel" at the end of verse 8, but a Hebrew manuscript from Qumran (4QDeut-j) reads "sons of God," most manuscripts of the Septuagint read "angels of God," and some read "sons of God." Many text critics regard the Qumran and Greek reading as original and the Masoretic reading as a later change that avoided the suggestion that God had divine subordinates (Tigay 1996; Heiser 2001; see also Ronald Hendel's discussion of 4QDeut-j in the Biblical Archaeology Review sidebar listed below). On this reading the verse describes the Most High assigning the nations to heavenly beings while keeping Israel as his own portion. Other scholars accept the textual argument but doubt that the verse implies a formal system of national patron deities, and others prefer the Masoretic reading, so the interpretation should be treated as probable but not settled. A related text is Deuteronomy 4:19-20, which says that the heavenly bodies were "allotted to all the peoples under the whole heaven," while Israel is the people the LORD took for himself.

Psalm 82 extends the picture:

"God has taken his place in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he holds judgment." — Psalm 82:1 (ESV)

The psalm accuses these "gods" of unjust judgment, announces that "like men you shall die" (Psalm 82:7), and ends with a prayer that God "inherit all the nations" (Psalm 82:8). Scholars have read the "gods" as human judges, as members of a heavenly assembly of the type known from the Ugaritic texts, or as the national patrons of Deuteronomy 32:8 (Mullen 1980; Smith 2001). The book of Daniel speaks of angelic "princes" of Persia and Greece (Daniel 10:13, 20; compare Collins 1993), and Sirach 17:17 and Jubilees 15:31-32 state explicitly that God set rulers or spirits over the nations but not over Israel. By the first century, therefore, a tradition of national patron angels was well established in Judaism, and Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6 in John 10:34.

The Old Testament also describes the worship of foreign gods as sacrifice to demons: "They sacrificed to demons that were no gods, to gods they had never known" (Deuteronomy 32:17), and "they sacrificed their sons and their daughters to the demons" (Psalm 106:37). The Septuagint reads Psalm 96:5 as "all the gods of the nations are demons," where the Hebrew calls them worthless idols.

The New Testament on idols and powers

Paul draws these strands together in 1 Corinthians. On the one hand "an idol has no real existence" and "there is no God but one" (1 Corinthians 8:4), and on the other hand, "there may be so-called gods in heaven or on earth—as indeed there are many 'gods' and many 'lords'—yet for us there is one God" (1 Corinthians 8:5-6). In 1 Corinthians 10:20 he adds that what the pagans sacrifice "they offer to demons and not to God." Galatians 4:8 speaks of those "that by nature are not gods," and Colossians 2:15 and Ephesians 6:12 speak of "rulers and authorities" and "cosmic powers" defeated or opposed by Christ and his church. The combination of statements has a consistent logic. The image is a created object, the "gods" it represents have no divine nature, and yet worship directed to them is not neutral and may be received by spiritual beings who are themselves created (Arnold 1992). Revelation 9:20 uses the same pairing when it speaks of "worshiping demons and idols."

Three explanations

These texts, together with the Greco-Roman evidence considered below, support three readings. The first, defended by Michael Heiser (2001; 2015) in a form he calls the divine council worldview, holds that spiritual beings stood behind the nations' gods. The second treats the gods as human inventions, and Wisdom 13-14 and Romans 1:19-25 give this explanation in terms of the human exchange of God's glory for created things. The third combines them: people made the images and myths, and spiritual powers exploited the worship, which is close to Paul's position in 1 Corinthians 8-10. The early Jewish and Christian writers help to show how each reading was applied.

Early Jewish and Christian explanations

Jewish authors of the Second Temple period used both kinds of explanation. In the Enochic tradition the fallen Watchers teach forbidden arts (1 Enoch 8), and the spirits of the Watchers lead people astray into sacrificing to demons as if they were gods (1 Enoch 19:1; compare Jubilees 11:4-5). Wisdom of Solomon 14:15-21, by contrast, derives idolatry from a father's grief, which makes an image of his dead child, and from the flattery of rulers. Maimonides, writing much later, gives a rationalist account in which the generation of Enosh erred by honouring the stars as God's servants (Laws of Idolatry 1:1).

Early Christian writers emphasised the demonic explanation. Justin Martyr (First Apology 5; Second Apology 5) taught that demons, born of the fallen angels, induced people to worship them through sacrifice and incense and that the poets, ignorant of this, attributed the demons' acts to the gods. Athenagoras (Plea for the Christians 24-26) and Tertullian (Apology 22) offer similar accounts, and Augustine (City of God 8-9) argues that the gods of the philosophers and of Rome are not true gods. At the same time Christian writers such as Lactantius used the euhemerist argument, derived from the Greek writer Euhemerus (c. 300 BC), that the gods began as deified kings. The pairing shows that the ancient church did not regard the two explanations as mutually exclusive.

The motif of divine beings who bring knowledge to humanity occurs more widely in the ancient world. Mesopotamian texts name seven antediluvian sages (apkallu) who taught civilisation, and Berossus (third century BC) tells of the half-fish Oannes who taught writing and the sciences. Scholars including Helge Kvanvig (2011) and Amar Annus (2010) argue that the Enochic account of the Watchers' teaching draws on this tradition. In Greek myth Prometheus plays a similar role. These comparisons are historically valuable, but they do not support claims about extraterrestrial visitors, which are rejected by historians of the ancient world.

Zeus and the Greek gods

The history of Zeus is long and relatively well documented. His name derives from the Indo-European sky god Dyēus, which also gave the Roman Jupiter, the Vedic Dyaus Pitar and the Germanic Tīwaz, and a god Zeus is named in Linear B tablets from the Mycenaean period (c. 1400-1200 BC). In Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BC) Zeus overthrows Cronus and the Titans and confines them in Tartarus, and a later battle with the Giants secures his rule (Burkert 1985). He is also the father of heroes such as Heracles and Perseus by mortal women.

The Jewish and Christian texts show contact with this material. The Septuagint renders Nephilim in Genesis 6:4 as gigantes, the Greek word for the Giants of myth. 2 Peter 2:4 uses the verb tartarōsas, derived from Tartarus, the Greek prison of the Titans, which occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. Bauckham (1983) argues that Jewish writers adapted this Greek imagery to describe the prison of the rebel angels, while others consider that the biblical tradition is older and that Greek myth preserves a distorted echo; the direction of influence cannot be determined with certainty.

The New Testament narrates three encounters with the Greek gods. At Lystra the crowd called Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes after a healing, and the priest of Zeus prepared sacrifices (Acts 14:8-18). At Athens Paul quoted the poet Aratus, "for we are indeed his offspring" (Acts 17:28), a line that originally addressed Zeus, and used it to argue that "we ought not to think that the divine being is like gold or silver or stone" before calling his hearers to repent (Acts 17:29-30). At Ephesus a crowd shouted "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" for two hours (Acts 19:28-34). Earlier, in 167 BC, Antiochus IV had set up an altar to Zeus Olympios in the temple at Jerusalem, an act connected with the "abomination of desolation" in 1 Maccabees 1:54, 2 Maccabees 6:2 and Daniel 11:31.

Odin and the Norse gods

The evidence for Odin is later and comes largely from outsiders and from Christian authors, which requires caution. The name Óðinn (Old English Woden, German Wotan) derives from a word meaning "fury" or "frenzy," and Wednesday is "Woden's day," a Germanic equivalent of the Roman dies Mercurii. About AD 98 Tacitus wrote that the Germans chiefly worshiped "Mercury," usually identified with Odin, and that on certain days they considered it right to offer human victims to him (Germania 9); his use of Roman names is an example of interpretatio Romana and not an independent report of Germanic names. Bede (Ecclesiastical History 1.15) says that the leaders Hengist and Horsa were descended from Woden, "from whose stock the royal families of many kingdoms" traced their line. Adam of Bremen (c. 1075) describes a temple at Uppsala with images of Thor, Wodan and Fricco and a festival every ninth year with animal and, he states, human sacrifice. The Poetic Edda, preserved in a manuscript of about 1270, describes Odin hanging nine nights on a tree, "given to Odin, myself to myself," to win the runes (Hávamál 138-139). The god of war, death, poetry and magic is thus attested over a thousand years, but the narrative sources are medieval (Davidson 1964; Turville-Petre 1964).

The Icelandic scholar Snorri Sturluson (c. 1220) gives an explanation in the prologue to his Prose Edda. He says that people "turned after the lusts of the world and slighted God's command," and then that a chieftain called Odin led the Æsir, "men of Asia," north and that they were honoured as being more like gods than men. This is a euhemerist account. Historians treat Snorri's genealogy as medieval learned reconstruction and not as history, but the passage shows how a Christian author of the thirteenth century could combine a human origin of the gods with a moral and spiritual fall.

Gods, kings and the "mighty men of old"

Genesis 6:4 calls the offspring of the sons of God "the mighty men who were of old, the men of renown," and the ancient world offers many examples of rulers who claimed divine descent. The Sumerian King List assigns kings before the flood reigns of tens of thousands of years, and the Epic of Gilgamesh calls its hero two-thirds god. Egyptian kings were sons of the sun god Re, and Greek heroes such as Heracles were children of gods and mortals. Alexander visited the oracle of Zeus-Ammon in 331 BC and was greeted as the god's son, Augustus took the title divi filius, and the Anglo-Saxon royal houses traced their descent to Woden.

Scholars explain these parallels in three ways. Some, such as Heiser (2015), see distorted memories of the events of Genesis 6 spread among the nations. Others treat the similarities as shared ancient Near Eastern ideas, or as Genesis deliberately answering the myths of its neighbours (Walton 2006; Hendel 1987), and still others stress the political function of divine descent in legitimising rulers. The parallels do not show that angels fathered the heroes, and any conclusion should be held with appropriate caution.

The biblical response: Christ and the powers

The New Testament presents the defeat of these powers as an accomplished fact and a promise yet to be completed. Psalm 82 ends with the prayer that God will inherit all the nations, and Jesus declares, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me," before sending his disciples to all nations (Matthew 28:18-19). Paul writes that Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Colossians 2:15), and that every knee will bow to him (Philippians 2:10). The scattering of Babel is reversed in the Pentecost narrative, where people from "every nation under heaven" hear the gospel in their own languages (Acts 2:5-11), in keeping with the promise to Abraham that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3), and the vision of Revelation 7:9 describes the final outcome.

Historians add a documented footnote. Christianity gradually replaced the cults described here: Roman emperors prohibited pagan sacrifice in the 390s, the Scandinavian kingdoms became Christian in the tenth and eleventh centuries, and Iceland adopted Christianity at its assembly in about AD 1000. This is a historical fact and does not itself establish a theological claim, although Christians read it as part of the fulfilment of the biblical promise that the nations would turn to the true God.

Conclusion

The three explanations of the gods can be summarised. The Bible attributes the worship of the nations to a combination of human error and spiritual deception, and the earliest Christian writers adopted both explanations in different proportions. The historical evidence for Zeus and Odin shows that the cults are well attested, that the sources for them are often late, and that the Greek and Norse worlds themselves contained both divine and human accounts of the gods' origin. A historian can document these beliefs and practices but cannot decide whether spiritual beings were involved, and a theologian who answers that question does so on the basis of Scripture and the church's reflection on it.

Read as a whole, the four studies trace one line of argument. Cain chose violence against God's warning, the corruption of Genesis 6 and the flood followed, the survivors repeated the pattern at Babel, and the nations then went their own ways, while the Bible records God calling a people for himself. The New Testament answers the sequence in Christ, whose blood "speaks a better word" (Hebrews 12:24), and who gathers the nations that Babel divided.
